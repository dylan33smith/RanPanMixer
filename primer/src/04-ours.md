## Section 4 — Our Mechanism

This section describes the mechanism this project is building. Its working title is **"Private Genome Path Release against a Public Pangenome Graph"**, and the durable source document for everything in Part A is the proposal PDF in this repository at `paper/Private_Genome_Path_Release.pdf`. Part A builds the theory from scratch. Part B compares it, step by step, with PanMixer (the published obfuscation tool dissected in Section 3; for readers arriving here first, PanMixer is an existing, published method that protects people who *contributed* to a pangenome graph by rewriting some of their haplotype blocks inside the released cohort file).

> **Question** — *"Three meanings for eps, three for p and q, three for n. Can we just rename ours so the collisions go away? Which names go in our paper, and do we have to keep theirs when we cite them?"*
>
> The primer has to carry both, because it reads both documents side by side and a reader holding the PanMixer paper needs its symbols to mean what the paper says. Our own write-up does not have that constraint and should pick one set.
>
> The proposal — and it is a **proposal, not a decision** — is to keep `tau`, `eta_tau = arctanh(tau)`, `epsilon_tau = 2·arctanh(tau)`, `R_D`, `Q_p`, `u(p, y)`, `phi_t`, `beta_t` and `psi_t` as ours, and to refer to PanMixer's quantities only by names that cannot collide: `eps_pmi` for its per-block privacy value (never bare `eps`), `eps_private` for its operating point, `eta_panmixer` or "the 1/support cost" for its utility cost, and `mu_mis` for its per-anchor mismatch rate. One forbidden alias regardless of the naming choice: **never call `tau` "the privacy budget"** — the budget is `epsilon_tau`, and `tau` is a bound on total variation distance.

> **Question** — *"The table says K = 88 for us because the target is external. Is K = 88 or K = 86? PanMixer uses 86 because it removes the target's own haplotypes."*
>
> For our mechanism it is **88**, unconditionally, and the primer must not blur that with the one place 86 appears.
>
> **Our mechanism runs at K = 88.** The target was never in the graph, so there is nothing to remove, and all 44 cohort individuals × 2 haplotypes are available as donor states (measured in this project for the cohort size). This is not a modelling choice we could have made differently — it follows from the target being external, and it is why condition 1 holds for us **by construction** (§4.4).
>
> **K = 86 belongs to one optional experiment: the head-to-head arm.** There a *cohort member stands in* as the target, so externality has to be **simulated**, and simulating it means removing that stand-in's two haplotypes: 2(N−1) = **86**. That number is a property of the benchmark, not of the design (see §4.16.1).
>
> So: 88 is what our guarantee is stated over and what the mechanism runs on; 86 is what a cohort-member stand-in forces on a comparison run. Note the standing limit on that comparison — the PGGB graph topology was built from all HPRC assemblies, so even a full leave-one-out only **approximates** the external-target setting on their data. That is a limit of the comparison arm, not of our design.

> **Question** — *"'Concatenate' has a symbol but an actual path is a node sequence. Does concatenating two local traversals always give a valid path, or can it produce something the graph does not allow?"*
>
> It can, in general, which is why §4.9 gives the model a way to forbid it: transitions with probability **zero** encode graph-incompatible concatenations, and `Y_D` is **defined** as what the model can emit, so a forbidden concatenation is outside the output support rather than a zero-probability member of it.
>
> For **v1** specifically the question does not arise in that form, and it is worth saying early because the symbol appears long before the explanation. v1 copies whole donor alleles at existing VCF rows, so every allele it writes is one a real donor carries at that row — which does not by itself prove the whole assembled row vector is a walk through the graph, but does mean v1 never invents an allele. Whether `Y_D` can be made to contain **only** graph-valid paths is recorded as an open question (§4.16.3, second **Caution**); PanMixer never checks it either, and the paper's hierarchical nested sampling is not implemented in the release (measured in this project).

## Part A — The theory of our mechanism

### 4.1 What kind of guarantee do we actually want?

Section 3 described how PanMixer is evaluated: build a specific attacker (the "gap-score" linkage attack, and in the published version an additional membership-inference analysis), run it against the obfuscated output, and show that it fails. That is an **empirical** guarantee. It is genuinely informative — an attack that succeeds is a proof of insecurity — but it has a structural weakness: it tells you nothing about the attacker you did not think of. A gap-score attack failing does not imply that a k-mer-based attack fails, or that an attacker with a better reference panel fails, or that an attacker five years from now with a larger public cohort fails. This is not a hypothetical worry in genomics: the result usually credited to **Homer and colleagues (2008)** showed that even *aggregate* allele frequencies — a summary that everyone had assumed was safe — could reveal whether a known individual was in a pool. That is standard background in the field rather than anything measured in this project, and it is the canonical example of an assumed-safe release later turning out to be attackable.

We want the other kind of guarantee: a **worst-case, attacker-independent** one. The trick for getting such a thing — and it is the central trick of the whole privacy literature — is to stop reasoning about attackers and start reasoning about **distributions**.

Here is why that works. Suppose the release mechanism is random. Then for each possible private input there is a *distribution* over what might come out. Call the distribution induced by input p the release distribution, written Q_p. An attacker who sees one released output and tries to decide "did this come from input p, or from input q?" is, mathematically, running a **binary hypothesis test** between two distributions, Q_p and Q_q. (A hypothesis test is just a rule that maps each possible observation to one of the two answers; its accuracy is the probability that the answer is right.) And there is a classical fact that caps how well *any* such rule can possibly do.

That fact is stated in terms of **total variation distance** (TV). For two probability distributions P and Q over the same finite set of outcomes Y_D,

TV(P, Q) = (1/2) · sum over y in Y_D of |P(y) − Q(y)|.

*In words:* line the two distributions up outcome by outcome, take the absolute gap at each outcome, add all the gaps up, and halve. TV is 0 when the two distributions are identical and 1 when they are completely disjoint (no outcome has positive probability under both). The proposal defines TV in its more general form as the supremum — the least upper bound — over all subsets A of Y_D of |P(A) − Q(A)|; on a finite outcome set like ours the supremum is attained, so "supremum over events" and "maximum over events" mean the same thing here, and both equal the half-sum-of-gaps formula above.

**Micro-example.** Three possible outputs y1, y2, y3. P = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2) and Q = (0.4, 0.4, 0.2). The gaps are 0.1, 0.1, 0.0; their sum is 0.2; TV = 0.1. Now be the attacker. You see one output and must guess which distribution produced it, knowing each was equally likely a priori (a **prior** is the attacker's belief before seeing any data; "equal priors" means 50/50). The best rule is to name whichever distribution gives the observed outcome higher probability: on y1 guess P (0.5 > 0.4), on y2 guess Q (0.4 > 0.3), and on y3 it is a tie (0.2 = 0.2), so any rule will do. Ties genuinely do not matter: whatever you do on y3, you are right with probability 0.5 · 0.2 / (0.5 · 0.2 + 0.5 · 0.2) = 1/2 of the times y3 occurs, so y3 contributes the same amount however you break it. Assigning y3 to P for bookkeeping, your success probability is 0.5 · P({y1, y3}) + 0.5 · Q({y2}) = 0.5 · 0.7 + 0.5 · 0.4 = **0.55**. And 0.55 = (1 + 0.1)/2 = (1 + TV)/2. This is not a coincidence; it is the general identity.

**The identity we will lean on:** under equal priors, the best achievable accuracy of *any* binary test distinguishing Q_p from Q_q is exactly (1 + TV(Q_p, Q_q))/2.

So if we can *prove* TV(Q_p, Q_q) ≤ tau for every pair of possible private inputs p and q, we have proved that no attacker of any kind, with unlimited compute and full knowledge of our algorithm, can beat accuracy (1 + tau)/2 at telling the target's release apart from anyone else's. That is the guarantee we are after. It is the content of Definition 1 in the proposal, which states it in two strengths: the weaker form asks only that the target be hard to distinguish from each of the n cohort members (max over i of TV(Q_p, Q_{p_i}) ≤ tau), and the stronger form asks it for *every* pair of admissible inputs. Our mechanism achieves the stronger form.


> **Question** — *"TV ≤ tau for every pair of admissible inputs — including pairs where neither path is a real human genome. Isn't that so pessimistic that the guarantee becomes unusable? That smells like the same problem as the beta budget."*
>
> It is the same tension seen from the other end, and saying so is the honest answer rather than a deflection. A guarantee quantified over a larger input set is a **stronger statement**, and it is also a **harder constraint to satisfy** — and §4.10.4 is where the constraint's price appears as a tilt factor indistinguishable from 1 at whole-chromosome scope.
>
> Two things make it less pessimistic than it sounds. The proof never uses **any** property of the input path beyond u(p, y) lying in [0, 1] (§4.7), so admitting biologically implausible paths costs nothing in the proof — it is free strength, not paid-for strength. And whether P(G) should be restricted is recorded as an **open question** rather than assumed away (§4.2), so the option of buying back budget by shrinking the input space is still on the table, with the caveat that any restriction must be made without looking at the target.

> **Question** — *"Work the micro-example again for the case where the two distributions are nearly identical — say TV = 0.01. What does 50.5% accuracy feel like in practice for an attacker who gets to try against 3,202 candidates?"*
>
> At TV = 0.01 the optimal **pairwise** attacker — told the release came from either p or q, and asked which — succeeds with probability (1 + 0.01)/2 = **0.505** against a coin's 0.500 (derived). That is the quantity the theorem bounds, and at tau = 0.01 the corresponding budget is epsilon_tau = 2·arctanh(0.01) = **0.020001** (derived).
>
> The ranking attack you are describing is a different experiment and the bound does not directly give its accuracy. An attacker holding 3,202 candidates computes or approximates Q_q for each candidate q, scores the observed release y under each, and ranks them. That attacker is not restricted to one pairwise comparison, and being top-1 out of 3,202 is a much weaker requirement than beating a coin.
>
> What the bound does give is every pairwise comparison inside that ranking: no candidate can be separated from the true one by more than the pairwise advantage. So the guarantee constrains the ranking without directly naming its top-1 rate — which is exactly why **measured attack success is the only head-to-head axis** and why the empirical ranking attack is run anyway (see §1.3.7 and §4.11). Note also that the (1 + tau)/2 identity assumes **equal priors**; an attacker with an informative prior over who the target might be is a different question this bound does not answer.

> **Question** — *"Definition 1 has a weak form (max over the n cohort members) and a strong form (every admissible pair). Which one does the theorem prove, and which one does our implementation deliver?"*
>
> The theorem proves the **strong** form: the bound holds for every pair of admissible input paths, not just for pairs drawn from the cohort. The mechanism as specified delivers the strong form too, because the tilt is bounded pointwise for every input — nothing in the argument looks at where p came from.
>
> v1 delivers it at the same scope the theorem is stated at: under the decided architecture (model C) there is **one chain over the whole chromosome**, so Theorem 1 applies to the whole released path directly, with nothing to factorise and no per-block normaliser to compose. (The model-B fallback would need the factorisation argument of §4.16.3; it holds there too, given that the baseline factorises over blocks, the beta sum to 1, and every phi lies in [0, 1].) What must be checked in code either way is bounded utility, and exit gate 1 checks it at toy scale — including that log Z_p lands inside [0, eta_tau] on every draw, and that tau = 0 reproduces the baseline exactly (§4.16.5).

### 4.2 The setting: what is public and what is private

Be precise about the threat model, because the guarantee is only as good as its assumptions.

- **G**, the pangenome graph, is **public**. It was built from the cohort **D = {g_1, …, g_n}**, which is also public — these are the HPRC assemblies, already released. In the data this project actually works with, the released cohort VCF carries 45 samples; one of them is **CHM13**, which is a *human* cell line derived from a complete hydatidiform mole (an abnormal human conception in which all chromosomes come from the father, so the resulting line is homozygous nearly everywhere — an excellent assembly substrate, but a reference assembly rather than a consenting cohort donor whose privacy is at stake). The pipeline drops CHM13 and the X chromosome, leaving **44 individuals = 88 haplotypes**, which is also the cohort size stated in the published PanMixer paper (measured in this project and cross-checked against the paper; see `docs/memory.md`, entries dated 2026-09-18).
- Each cohort genome g_i has a path p_i = Map(g_i, G) through the graph. These paths are **public** too: they are literally in the graph.
- **g** is an external target genome that is **not** in D. This is the crucial structural difference from PanMixer and we will return to it repeatedly. The target did not contribute to the graph; the graph knows nothing about them.
- **p = Map(g, G)** is the target's mapped path. **This, and only this, is private.**
- The attacker knows G, D, all the cohort paths, our entire algorithm, our utility function, and the value of tau. The only thing they do not know is the outcome of our random number generator. (This is Kerckhoffs's principle from cryptography, imported wholesale: assume the enemy knows the system, keep only the key — here, the randomness — secret.)

Two more objects:

- **P(G)**, the *admissible input-path space*. The proposal introduces this set without pinning it down, so state a concrete reading and flag it: for our purposes an admissible path is **any valid traversal of G** — a sequence of nodes in which every consecutive pair is joined by an edge — regardless of whether any cohort member happens to carry it. This matters because Theorem 1 is quantified over all p, q in P(G); the larger P(G) is, the stronger the theorem, and the reader cannot judge its strength without knowing what is in there. **Open question:** whether the implementation should restrict P(G) further (for example to paths a real mapper could actually produce), and whether doing so would weaken the guarantee's interpretation.
- **Y_D**, the *output support*: the fixed set of paths the mechanism is permitted to emit, a subset of P(G). The proposal allows several choices — the complete cohort haplotype paths, synthetic paths from a cohort haplotype model, mosaics of cohort-supported local traversals, or graph-valid paths generated by an HMM. ("Mosaic" here means a path stitched together from segments of several different cohort haplotypes, the way a real chromosome is a mosaic of its ancestors' chromosomes because of recombination.) The requirement is that **Y_D is chosen without looking at the target.**


> **Question** — *"Our target is external — but what if they're a relative of a cohort member? Section 2 said relatives leak. Does tau still hold, and does it still mean anything?"*
>
> tau still holds, and its **operational reading weakens**. Those are two different statements and the difference is the whole answer.
>
> The bound is quantified over pairs of input paths and says nothing about how the attacker's prior was formed, so relatedness cannot violate it. But the headline reading — "an optimal attacker succeeds with probability at most (1 + tau)/2" — is an **equal-priors** statement (project definition, `docs/terms.md`). An attacker who knows the target is a first-degree relative of a named cohort member starts from a prior nowhere near 50/50, and the same tau then buys a smaller *absolute* improvement over a prior that was already informative.
>
> Section 2 supplies the scale of the prior: about **half** the genome is shared with a parent, sibling or child and about an **eighth** with a first cousin, in long detectable segments. So a relative is not an edge case to note and move past — it is the ordinary case in which a bounded mechanism still leaves an attacker well ahead of chance, for reasons the mechanism was never claiming to fix.

> **Question** — *"P(G) is 'any valid traversal'. That includes paths no human could carry. Why would we want the theorem quantified over nonsense inputs — does making P(G) bigger make the guarantee stronger, or just harder to satisfy?"*
>
> Stronger, and here it is also free. Stronger because a bound over a larger set of inputs implies the bound over any subset. Free because the proof's only use of p is through u(p, y), which is bounded in [0, 1] by construction — no step needs p to be biologically plausible, so admitting implausible paths adds no cost to the argument.
>
> Where it stops being free is if a restriction would buy something back. Restricting P(G) to plausible paths is recorded as an **open question**, with the standing constraint that any such restriction must be defined **without looking at the target** — a target-pruned input space is exactly the disjoint-support failure §4.4 works through.

> **Question** — *"'p = Map(g, G)' — what tool is Map? If the target hands us a FASTQ, who runs the mapper, and is the mapper's output deterministic?"*
>
> `Map` is graph alignment — in practice something like `vg giraffe`, the same aligner the read-mapping evaluation uses (see Step 26). Whoever holds the target's reads runs it, and the result is the target's path p through the public graph. That step is upstream of the mechanism: our guarantee starts from p and says nothing about how p was produced.
>
> On determinism, the important point is a warning rather than a fact: whatever nondeterminism a mapper has is **not** the mechanism's randomness and must never be relied on for privacy. The privacy comes from one place only — the draw from Q_p — and treating mapper jitter as if it contributed would be the same category error as treating PanMixer's seed as a guarantee (see §A.7).

### 4.3 The central idea: do not edit, resample

PanMixer's release rule is *editing*: take the target's own haplotype and overwrite some blocks. Our release rule is fundamentally different:

> **Key idea** — We never edit the target's path. We **draw a brand-new path** from a probability distribution over cohort-supported paths, and we release that.

The target's path influences *which* path gets drawn — otherwise the output would be useless — but only by **re-weighting** the probabilities, and only by a **bounded** amount. Nothing of the target is ever copied through unchanged by construction; every symbol in the output is a symbol that came from a cohort-supported candidate.

This is what makes a worst-case bound reachable. If the output is always drawn from the same fixed menu, then two different inputs produce two distributions over *the same menu*, and we can hope to bound the distance between them. If instead the output contains verbatim copies of the input, then two inputs that differ produce outputs that differ with certainty, and no bound is possible. (Part B, §4.12, makes this argument precise against PanMixer.)


> **Question** — *"If every symbol in the output comes from a cohort-supported candidate, and the target carries a novel variant nobody in the cohort has, then the release simply cannot contain it. Is that utility loss, or is it a correctness bug? What does a downstream user think they're looking at?"*
>
> It is by design, and it is the price of the guarantee rather than a bug — but the reason is worth stating precisely, because it is the same argument as §4.4's counterexample. If the output support could contain a symbol that **only** the target carries, then two inputs differing in exactly that symbol would produce releases with **disjoint** support, and total variation distance would be 1. A cohort-supported output is not a modelling convenience; it is what makes any bound possible at all.
>
> The scale of what is lost is measurable on PanMixer's own data: **56,889 chr21 variants (16.7%)** are novel in the sense that they appear in neither the reference panel nor the PanGenie callset, and at **11,760** chr21 sites the target HG00438 carries a non-REF allele whose median self-information is **5.41 nats** (all measured in this project). Those are exactly the sites a cohort-supported release cannot reproduce for a target whose novelty is genuinely private.
>
> So a downstream user must be told what the artefact is: **a cohort-supported path that resembles the target's, not the target's path**. It is suitable for questions about how a genome of this kind maps against this graph, and unsuitable for any question about the target's private novel sequence. The mandatory companion measurement is `target_fidelity` — how much of the true target the release retains — without which a maximally private release looks like a free lunch on every cohort-level metric (see the comparison table in §4.11).

> **Question** — *"PanMixer at capacity 1.0 would replace every block. Is our mechanism just 'PanMixer at capacity 1.0 with a calibrated sampler', or is it genuinely different?"*
>
> Genuinely different, for two reasons that are independent of each other.
>
> First, capacity 1.0 does not replace every block. The knapsack **saturates**: at capacity 0.5 it already spends only **33.1%** of its denominator, because every move with positive value has been bought and a move with `eps_j` = 0 is never worth buying at any budget (measured in this project). Raising capacity does not reach the blocks whose privacy value is zero — including the `CONFLICT`-affected records, which are worth zero and cost **1.60×** more (measured in this project).
>
> Second, and decisively, even if every block were replaced the **selection pattern** remains a function of the target, and the conditional structure leaks (§4.12). There is also no dial connecting the two mechanisms: PanMixer's candidate is an **unconditional prior draw** with no comparison to the original and no rejection loop (measured in this project, reading `sample_block_prior`), so there is no setting of capacity at which its candidate generator becomes a tilted draw. The privacy machinery is in a different place — theirs in selection, ours in the sampler.

> **Question** — *"Does 'resample everything' mean our output has strictly worse utility than theirs for every downstream use? If so, what's the pitch?"*
>
> The first half has to be answered honestly and the answer is that **we do not know, because nothing has been measured at any value of tau**. Every PanMixer headline figure is recorded in this project as unrun: eps_private = **0.002**, average utility loss **0.28**, allele-frequency divergence **0.006 / 0.006 / 0.005**, LD divergence **0.003**, obfuscated read mapping **77.82 / 95.61 / 77.01** (all quoted from the published paper; **we have reproduced none of them**). On our side there is no `target_fidelity` number, no attacker accuracy, no empirical total variation distance, at any tau. Anybody who tells you the utility comparison is settled in either direction is ahead of the data.
>
> The second half is where the interesting content is, because **"utility" as PanMixer measures it is the wrong axis for this comparison**, and this is the single most useful thing to take out of §4.3. Two of their three headline utility metrics — allele-frequency divergence (`af_loss`) and LD divergence (`ld_loss`) — are **cohort-distortion** metrics: they ask how much overwriting one member's genotype column moved the released cohort's own statistics. That question is well posed for PanMixer and it is **not defined for our release object at all**, because we never edit the cohort; they are not metrics this project computes (`docs/terms.md`). The third, read-mapping rate, does transfer, and it is cohort-level in the weaker sense that a pure prior draw from R_D with **no tilt at all** is a real, cohort-plausible haplotype that maps well while carrying **zero** information about the target. That is our tau = 0 point, and on any scorecard made of cohort-plausibility measures it would look like a free lunch. Which is exactly why `target_fidelity` is recorded as a **PRIMARY, mandatory** axis for any head-to-head (project decision, `docs/plan.md` and `docs/terms.md`), and why the comparison is only meaningful **at matched empirical attack success**, never metric-against-metric.
>
> Where we should genuinely expect to lose is on similarity to the target at a low operating point, and the mechanism is plain: at capacity 0.1 PanMixer changes **68,335 of 650,541** allele slots, **10.50%**, leaving about **89.5%** of the target's own alleles in the release verbatim (measured in this project; the complement is derived). An output that is 89.5% the target's own data will beat a fully resampled one on anything that measures resemblance to the target — but it earns that score by publishing the material, which is the same fact §4.12 turns into a total variation distance of 1.
>
> So the pitch is not "better utility". It is: **a bound that holds for every admissible pair of inputs and every attacker, against a threshold measured against one attacker on one attack** (§4.1, §4.11) — and then, once measured, whatever utility we retain *at matched attack success*. The head-to-head that decides it is specified but not run: both arms emitting `new_haplotypes` through the shared evaluators, **R ≥ 10 seeds per (subject, level)** in each arm, and attacks run **per operating point rather than pooled**, because a frontier of *k* points is *k* correlated releases of one genome and our guarantee is one release per genome (project decision, `docs/plan.md`). Until that runs, the honest claim is about the *shape* of the guarantee, not about utility.

### 4.4 Ingredient 1: the target-independent baseline, R_D

**R_D** is a probability distribution over the output set Y_D, built **only** from the public graph G and the public cohort D. Think of it as a generative model of "what a plausible human chromosome looks like, according to the cohort" — the same kind of object as the Li–Stephens copying HMM that PanMixer uses (Section 3), but put to a different purpose. Two requirements from the proposal:

1. **R_D(y) > 0 for every y in Y_D.** No output the mechanism could emit is assigned probability zero. (If some output had probability zero under the baseline but positive probability under a tilt, likelihood ratios would blow up.)
2. **R_D does not depend on the target in any way.**

**Why requirement 2 matters — a worked counterexample.** Imagine we cheated and let R_D depend on the target: say we built the baseline HMM from D *plus the target*, or we pruned Y_D to "paths within 5% of the target." Now consider two possible targets, Alice and Bob, whose paths differ at exactly one rare variant. Alice's pruned menu contains paths carrying that rare allele; Bob's does not. An attacker who sees an output carrying the rare allele knows instantly it was Alice. The supports are disjoint on that slice, TV = 1, and the guarantee is gone — not weakened, *gone*. This is exactly the failure mode the proposal's "common support" condition rules out.

Note a pleasant consequence of our threat model: because the target is **external** to D, every quantity derived from the graph (variant support counts, cohort haplotypes, the transition constants) is automatically target-independent. We get condition 2 **by construction** — K = 88, nothing is held out, and there is nothing to audit for target inclusion in the mechanism's own inputs. PanMixer cannot get it that way, because its target is *inside* the cohort — which is precisely the origin of released-code departure (c), where the privacy scorer scores the target against a panel that contains the target. We return to the measured size of that effect in §4.13.


> **Question** — *"R_D must not depend on the target. But the LD blocks come from the 1000 Genomes panel, and our target might be in 1000 Genomes. Does that break condition 2?"*
>
> Make one distinction first, because it decides the answer. Condition 2 forbids R_D from depending on the target's **private input path p**. A panel that happens to contain the target's **genotypes** is not the same failure — it is a dependence on the target's *data*, which is worse rather than better, because it means the baseline was partly estimated from the very person the baseline is supposed to be innocent of.
>
> Two things then have to be kept apart, because the audit finding behind this question is real and the conclusion people draw from it usually is not.
>
> **The deployed mechanism.** Under the decided chain architecture (model C, §4.16) the chain unit is the individual variant, so there are **no LD blocks in the mechanism**, no PLINK run, no allele-frequency table, and **no external panel in the mechanism's input set at all**. Its inputs are `G`, the cohort allele matrix, the GRCh38 genetic map and the target's path. A panel that does or does not contain the target cannot break condition 2 for an object that never reads a panel, and the target is external to `G` and `D` besides. Condition 2 holds by construction.
>
> **The artifacts this project actually built.** The finding stands and must not be softened: our 30x repin put **39 of 44** HPRC donors, including HG00438, into the panel that branch 1 of PanMixer's frequency table counts from, against **0 of 44** under Phase 3 (measured in this project). That is true of the pipeline as run, and it matters — but it is a property of the **PanMixer arm and the head-to-head benchmark**, which do read that panel, not of our sampler, which does not. The fix is adaptation 1 of §4.14, and it is scoped to those arms.
>
> The distinction to carry out of this: target-freedom is something a **benchmark** has to be rebuilt and asserted into, and something the **deployed mechanism** gets from the threat model. Where leave-one-out is needed — donor panel, support counts, site list, attack database — it is needed for the head-to-head arm alone, where a cohort member stands in as target (project definition, `docs/terms.md`, `loo_cohort`). Recomputing only the donor panel, which is what PanMixer does, is not a leave-one-out cohort even there.

> **Question** — *"The pruning counterexample is good. Can you do the same counterexample for the baseline rather than the support — build R_D from D plus the target and show me where the proof breaks?"*
>
> Take the proof's Step 3 (§4.7), which is where target-independence is load-bearing. The likelihood ratio is
>
> **Q_p(y)/Q_q(y) = [R_D(y)·exp(eta_tau·u(p,y))/Z_p] / [R_D(y)·exp(eta_tau·u(q,y))/Z_q]**
>
> and the whole bound rests on R_D(y) **cancelling**, leaving only a bounded tilt ratio and a bounded Z ratio.
>
> Now let the baseline depend on the input: write R_p and R_q instead of one R_D. The ratio becomes [R_p(y)/R_q(y)] · (bounded tilt ratio), and the first factor is unbounded in general. Concretely: build the baseline by adding the target's own haplotypes to the donor panel. Then for an input p that carries a private allele at some site, R_p puts positive mass on outputs carrying that allele, while for a q that does not carry it, R_q puts mass **zero** there. The ratio is infinite, the supports are disjoint at that site, and total variation distance is **1** — the bound is gone, and no value of tau recovers it. That is the same failure as the pruned-menu counterexample, arriving through the baseline rather than through the support. Note where the danger actually lies for us: our target is external, so no cohort-derived baseline contains their haplotypes and this counterexample describes something we would have to *do on purpose*. It is a live hazard only in the head-to-head arm, where a cohort member stands in as target and the stand-in's two haplotypes must be removed for the simulation of externality to mean anything.

> **Question** — *"R_D(y) > 0 for every y in Y_D. With a stay/switch HMM over 88 donors, is every path really reachable? What about a path that needs a transition the graph forbids?"*
>
> The requirement is satisfied by how Y_D is **defined**, not by a property of the transition matrix. Y_D is the set of outputs the baseline model can emit; a concatenation the graph forbids is given transition probability zero and is therefore **not a member of Y_D** rather than a zero-probability member of it. Strict positivity is a statement about the members.
>
> Practically, with a stay/switch matrix every donor is reachable at every position — the switch probability is small but nonzero, which is precisely the property PanMixer's shipped constants destroy (switch mass **5.8 × 10⁻¹²** at a one-index gap, and **300 of 300** real blocks returning a single donor copied verbatim, both measured in this project). If a missing-data policy were implemented by dropping donors at some positions, that changes Y_D itself, which is allowed only because the policy is fixed before the target is seen — a point §4.8 has to make explicitly, and §4.17 is where the policy itself is set out.

### 4.5 Ingredient 2: a bounded utility function, u(p, y)

**u(p, y)** is a number between 0 and 1 saying how well output y serves input p. Larger is better. u = 1 means "perfect for this target"; u = 0 means "useless for this target."

The proposal's blockwise construction: cut the path into **T** ordered blocks (LD blocks, graph blocks, or anchor sites — the block segmentation described in Section 3 works fine here), then

u(p, y) = sum over t = 1..T of beta_t · phi_t(p_t, y_t)

where:

- **p_t** is the input path restricted to block t; **y_t** is the output path restricted to block t.
- **phi_t(p_t, y_t)** is a per-block similarity in [0, 1]. 1 = the output block is perfect for the target at this block, 0 = worthless.
- **beta_t ≥ 0** is a per-block importance weight, and the weights sum to 1 across all T blocks: sum of beta_t = 1.

*In words:* score each block separately on a 0-to-1 scale, then take a weighted average of those scores, with the weights saying how much each block matters.

Because each phi_t is in [0, 1] and the beta_t are non-negative and sum to 1, u is a **weighted average of numbers in [0, 1]**, so u itself is automatically in [0, 1]. That is the whole point of the construction — boundedness is structural, not something we have to check case by case.

**A concrete phi_t.** The proposal suggests a population-weighted k-mer agreement. A **k-mer** is a length-k substring of DNA; K(a) denotes the set of k-mers appearing in sequence a. The notation 1[x in K(a)] is an **indicator**: it is 1 if k-mer x occurs in a, and 0 otherwise. Then

phi_t(a, b) = [sum over x of w(x)·min(1[x in K(a)], 1[x in K(b)])] / [sum over x of w(x)·max(1[x in K(a)], 1[x in K(b)])]

*In words:* the min is 1 only when the k-mer is in *both* sequences, and the max is 1 when it is in *either*, so the numerator is the weighted size of the intersection and the denominator the weighted size of the union. This is a **weighted Jaccard index**: weighted-intersection over weighted-union, which is 1 for identical k-mer sets and 0 for disjoint ones. The weight w(x) downweights k-mers that appear in everybody (uninformative) and upweights population-informative ones.

**Micro-example.** K(a) = {x1, x2, x3}, K(b) = {x2, x3, x4}, with weights w(x1) = 3, w(x2) = 1, w(x3) = 1, w(x4) = 5. The intersection is {x2, x3} with total weight 2. The union is {x1, x2, x3, x4} with total weight 10. So phi = 2/10 = 0.2.

**A concrete beta_t.** Section 3 described PanMixer's per-variant utility weight: each variant v carries weight 1/support(v), where support(v) is the number of cohort haplotypes traversing v — so rare variants, traversed by few haplotypes, are worth more. §4.13 below turns that raw weight into our beta_t and phi_t.


> **Question** — *"The k-mer phi_t needs sequence. Our data model is an integer allele-index matrix with no sequence in it at all. Where do the k-mers come from — do we have to go back to the graph?"*
>
> You would have to go back to the graph, and **v1 does not do it.** Say both halves plainly, because the illustration in this section and the thing v1 will run are not the same object.
>
> The k-mer weighted Jaccard needs node sequences, which means resolving each allele index back to the sequence the graph's nodes carry. The matrix from Step 9 holds only `X[i, s, h]` — an allele **index** per record per haplotype, with REF as 0 — and the converter discards REF and ALT strings entirely, along with the whole `INFO` column (measured in this project). So there is no sequence anywhere in the data model the mechanism operates on.
>
> v1's phi_t is instead the construction derived from PanMixer's per-variant weight: W_t = sum over the block's variants of 1/support(v), with beta_t = W_t / sum_b W_b and phi_t the fraction of that weight the output reproduces (§4.13). That keeps everything inside the integer matrix. phi_t is otherwise **entirely planned** — the k-mer Jaccard is the proposal's worked example, not an implemented choice, and there is no decision yet on k, on the weighting function, or on set versus multiset semantics.
>
> One constraint that any candidate phi_t must satisfy, and that this section does not yet state: **phi_t must decompose along the chain positions inside a block.** A whole-block similarity that does not decompose cannot be sampled exactly by FFBS (§4.16.4). A k-mer Jaccard over a whole block's sequence does not decompose, which is a second reason v1 does not use it.

> **Question** — *"Work the weighted Jaccard once more with a case where the two sets overlap on the heavy k-mer instead of the light ones, so I can see the weighting actually doing something. And w(x) 'downweights k-mers that appear in everybody' — computed from which dataset?"*
>
> Weighted Jaccard is the weight of the intersection over the weight of the union. Take the target's block to contribute k-mers {A, B, C} with weights w(A) = 5 (rare, therefore heavy), w(B) = 1 and w(C) = 1.
>
> - Output contains {A, D}, with w(D) = 1. Intersection {A}, weight **5**; union {A, B, C, D}, weight **8**; **phi_t = 5/8 = 0.625** (derived).
> - Output contains {B, C} instead. Intersection {B, C}, weight **2**; union {A, B, C}, weight **7**; **phi_t = 2/7 = 0.286** (derived).
>
> So matching the single heavy k-mer scores more than twice as well as matching both light ones, which is the weighting doing its job: the utility function is being told that reproducing rare material matters more than reproducing common material.
>
> On the dataset question: the weights must come from **public cohort or panel data and nothing containing the target**, for the same reason as §4.4 — a weight estimated from the target's own sequence makes u depend on p through a channel the proof does not bound, and then condition 2 is violated in a place nobody is looking. Which dataset it should be is not decided.

> **Question** — *"Boundedness is 'structural, not something we have to check'. But the §4.13 construction divides by W_t. What if a block has W_t = 0 — every variant in it has support 0?"*
>
> Under **model C** there is no per-block W_t to divide by — a chain position is one variant, phi_v is an indicator, and beta_v = w_v / Σ_u w_u with w_v = 1/support(v) — so the degenerate case narrows to a single position with **zero** support, where there is no called haplotype to compare against at all. That still has to be handled by construction rather than discovered at runtime: such a position contributes nothing, gets beta_v = 0, and is **excluded from the normalisation**, because weights that no longer sum to 1 break the calibration rather than merely the emphasis. (Under the model-B fallback the same guard is needed one level up, for a block in which every site has zero support.)
>
> How close the real data comes to it, and it is now measured rather than guessed: **exactly 1 of 340,824 chr21 sites** has all 88 donors missing, and **89 sites** leave one donor or fewer (measured in this project, 2026-09-28). For context, chr21 missingness is **4.26%** of matrix entries overall, **15.5%** of sites have at least one missing entry, and the mean number of missing haplotypes at a non-`CONFLICT` site is **3.6 of 88**, rising to **29.4 of 88** at a `CONFLICT` record (all measured in this project). So support is frequently reduced and almost never zero — but "almost never" is not "never" across 340,824 rows, and this wants a guard rather than a research programme.

### 4.6 The exponential tilt

Now combine them. Fix tau in [0, 1) and define the **tilt strength**

**eta_tau = arctanh(tau) = (1/2)·log[(1 + tau)/(1 − tau)]**.

(arctanh is the inverse hyperbolic tangent: the function that undoes tanh. For now just treat it as a number that grows smoothly from 0 to infinity as tau grows from 0 to 1. §4.7 explains why *this* function and no other.)

The release distribution for input path p is

**Q_p(y) = R_D(y) · exp(eta_tau · u(p, y)) / Z_p**, for y in Y_D,

where **Z_p = sum over z in Y_D of R_D(z)·exp(eta_tau · u(p, z))** is the normalizer that makes the probabilities sum to 1.

**In plain English:** start with the public baseline probability of each candidate output. Multiply it by a bonus factor that is larger for candidates that are more useful to the target. Renormalize so the numbers are probabilities again. That is all.

The bonus factor exp(eta_tau · u) ranges over exactly [1, exp(eta_tau)], because u ranges over [0, 1] and exp is increasing. So the *worst* candidate keeps its baseline weight untouched and the *best* candidate is multiplied by at most exp(eta_tau). The tilt cannot change any candidate's weight relative to any other by more than a factor of exp(eta_tau). **That single sentence is the entire privacy argument**; everything else is bookkeeping.

The two extremes are worth internalizing:

- **tau = 0** gives eta_tau = 0, so the bonus factor is exp(0) = 1 for every candidate, so Q_p = R_D for *every* input p. The output is a pure public cohort sample that does not depend on the target at all. Perfect privacy, zero target-specific utility.
- **tau → 1** gives eta_tau → infinity, so the mechanism concentrates arbitrarily hard on the highest-utility candidate. Maximum utility, no privacy guarantee.

tau is the dial between those two, and it is chosen **before** looking at any data.


> **Question** — *"At tau = 0.5 the best candidate gets at most a 1.7321× boost and the worst gets 1.0. With 88 donors starting near uniform, does a 1.73× boost on one of them even change the draw much? Work it for K = 88."*
>
> Work it exactly. At tau = 0.5, eta_tau = arctanh(0.5) = 0.549306 and the maximum bonus factor is exp(eta_tau) = **1.7321** (derived). Start from a uniform draw over 88 donors: each has probability 1/88 = **0.011364**. Give one donor the full bonus and leave the other 87 at 1. Its probability becomes
>
> **1.7321 / (87 + 1.7321) = 1.7321 / 88.7321 = 0.019521** (derived).
>
> So the favoured donor goes from about **1.14%** to about **1.95%** — a relative improvement of 1.73× and an absolute improvement of 0.8 percentage points. It is a real nudge and it is nowhere near a selection.
>
> That is the honest picture at the **best case for the tilt**, which is a single block getting the whole budget. §4.10.4 is where it gets worse: with uniform weights on chr21's T = 100,757 blocks the per-block factor is exp(eta_tau/T) = **1.0000055** (derived), at which point the draw is indistinguishable from the untilted baseline. The question you are asking here is the right one and the answer there is the reason the budget allocation is an open research question rather than a parameter.

> **Question** — *"tau = 0 gives Q_p = R_D for every input. So at tau = 0 we release a random cohort-like path that has nothing to do with the target. Is that even a release of the target's data? What would a reviewer say we had published?"*
>
> A reviewer would say, correctly, that you published a sample from a public model — and that is exactly what it is. At tau = 0 the bonus factor is exp(0) = 1 for every candidate, so Q_p = R_D identically, the private input never enters, and epsilon_tau = 0 (derived). The output is a cohort-supported path that carries **zero** information about the target.
>
> Name it as what it is: the natural analogue of PanMixer's **removal baseline** — the paper's "Removed" arm, where the target is excluded from the graph entirely, reported at Wasserstein **0.027** and LD **0.017** (both quoted from the published paper; **we have reproduced neither**). It is not the same object, though, and §4.16.2 keeps the two apart: theirs emits nothing for that subject, ours emits a cohort path drawn from R_D.
>
> It is also why `target_fidelity` is **mandatory** in any comparison: a tau = 0 release is a genuine cohort haplotype, so it would map as well as any real one while being useless about the target, and a floor column is the only thing that stops our maximal-privacy point from looking like a free lunch (project decision, `docs/memory.md`). Note which metrics that argument is and is not made with: read mapping transfers to our release object, whereas `af_loss` and `ld_loss` are cohort-distortion metrics that are not defined for a release which never edits the cohort (`docs/terms.md`).

> **Question** — *"'tau is chosen before looking at any data' — chosen by whom, and written down where? If we tune tau after seeing utility results, have we broken the theorem?"*
>
> The theorem is about a fixed tau, so what matters is **what** you tuned against. Tuning tau against **target-specific** outcomes — this target's attack results, this target's fidelity — makes the released distribution depend on the private input through a channel outside the proof, and that does break the guarantee. Informing a **policy** choice of tau from cohort-level utility curves computed without reference to any particular target does not.
>
> What we intend is the second, and the discipline that goes with it is pre-registration: tau is **chosen and pre-registered before any attack outcome is inspected**, and the utility function must be fixed before inspecting attack outcomes as well (project constraint). Who chooses it is not a technical question — what tau is defensible is recorded as an **open policy question** that this project can inform and cannot settle (§4.16.8, item 6), with tau = 0.10 bounding equal-prior identification at **0.55** as the worked reference point (derived). **No value of tau has been run end to end in this project.**

### 4.7 Why arctanh? Walking through the proof

The proof in the proposal is four lines. Here it is slowly.

**Step 1 — the bonus factor is bounded.** Since 0 ≤ u(p, y) ≤ 1, we have 1 ≤ exp(eta_tau · u(p, y)) ≤ exp(eta_tau). *(Exponentiating a number between 0 and eta_tau gives a number between 1 and e^eta_tau.)*

**Step 2 — the normalizer is bounded.** Z_p is a weighted average of those bonus factors, weighted by R_D (whose weights sum to 1 because it is a probability distribution). An average of numbers all lying in [1, exp(eta_tau)] must itself lie in [1, exp(eta_tau)]. So **1 ≤ Z_p ≤ exp(eta_tau)** for *every* input p. Consequently, for any two inputs p and q, the ratio Z_q/Z_p is at most (biggest possible Z_q)/(smallest possible Z_p) = exp(eta_tau)/1, and at least 1/exp(eta_tau): exp(−eta_tau) ≤ Z_q/Z_p ≤ exp(eta_tau).

**Step 3 — write down the likelihood ratio.** For any output y,

Q_p(y) / Q_q(y) = exp(eta_tau · [u(p, y) − u(q, y)]) · (Z_q / Z_p).

The R_D(y) factor is identical in numerator and denominator and cancels — *this is why the baseline must be target-independent.* If R_D depended on the target, the two R_D factors would be different functions and would not cancel, and there would be no bound at all.

**Step 4 — bound it.** Since both utilities lie in [0, 1], their difference lies in [−1, 1], so the first factor lies in [exp(−eta_tau), exp(eta_tau)]. Multiplying by the bound on Z_q/Z_p from Step 2:

**exp(−2·eta_tau) ≤ Q_p(y)/Q_q(y) ≤ exp(2·eta_tau)**, for every output y.

**Step 5 — convert a likelihood-ratio bound into a TV bound.** This is the standard step and it is where arctanh earns its keep. If two distributions have pointwise likelihood ratio everywhere within [exp(−s), exp(s)], then TV ≤ (exp(s) − 1)/(exp(s) + 1), and that expression is exactly tanh(s/2). Here s = 2·eta_tau, so TV ≤ tanh(eta_tau). And we *chose* eta_tau = arctanh(tau), so tanh(eta_tau) = tanh(arctanh(tau)) = **tau**.

That is the answer to "why arctanh": the likelihood-ratio-to-TV conversion is a tanh, so to land on exactly tau we invert the tanh. The calibration is not a heuristic; it is the exact inverse of the conversion.

**Theorem 1 (from the proposal).** For any two admissible input paths p and q in P(G): TV(Q_p, Q_q) ≤ tau, and moreover exp(−epsilon_tau) ≤ Q_p(y)/Q_q(y) ≤ exp(epsilon_tau) for every y, with **epsilon_tau = 2·arctanh(tau) = log[(1 + tau)/(1 − tau)]**.

> **Caution** — the proposal PDF, `paper/Private_Genome_Path_Release.pdf`, carries two typesetting slips at exactly this point, and they are worth knowing before you ever transcribe the theorem into code. In the statement of Theorem 1, and again in the first line of its proof, a "≤ e" has been lost before a superscript, so each two-sided bound prints as if it were one-sided: the theorem shows e^(−ε) ≤ Q_p(y) / Q_q(y) with the upper half's exponent left floating beside the fraction, and the proof's first line shows 1 ≤ exp(eta_tau · u(p, y)) with the same stray superscript. The intended forms are **e^(−ε) ≤ Q_p(y) / Q_q(y) ≤ e^(ε)**, where ε is epsilon_tau, and 1 ≤ exp(eta_tau · u(p, y)) ≤ e^(eta_tau) — which is how this section writes them. **The mathematics is unaffected**: the chain still yields TV ≤ tanh(eta_tau) = tau, and epsilon_tau = 2·arctanh(tau) is printed correctly elsewhere in the proposal. The reason it matters anyway is practical: a one-sided bound transcribed literally into a runtime assertion passes on every input, because the missing half is the half that can fail. That is why the log Z_p check of exit gate 1 (§4.16.5) is written as a two-sided bracket, [0, eta_tau], rather than as a single inequality. (Found by rendering page 3 of the PDF at 300 dpi and reading the image, not by text extraction, which mangles superscripts; `docs/memory.md`, 2026-08-29.)

**Corollary (attacker success).** Combining with the identity from §4.1: under equal priors, no binary attacker can exceed accuracy **(1 + tau)/2** at distinguishing the target's release from any other input's release.

**Corollary 1 of the proposal (cohort mixture).** Let pi = (pi_1, …, pi_n) be any public prior over the n cohort members (non-negative numbers summing to 1) and let Q_D be the mixture distribution "pick a cohort member at random according to pi, then run the mechanism on their path": Q_D = sum_i pi_i · Q_{p_i}. Then TV(Q_p, Q_D) ≤ tau as well. The proof uses the **convexity of total variation distance**, which is the inequality

TV(P, sum_i pi_i·Q_i) ≤ sum_i pi_i·TV(P, Q_i).

*In words:* blending distributions together cannot push the blend further from P than the worst ingredient is. Since each TV(Q_p, Q_{p_i}) ≤ tau, the weighted average of those is also ≤ tau. **Interpretation:** the target's release is hard to distinguish not only from any *specific* cohort member's release but also from a release generated by a randomly chosen cohort member — i.e., the target blends into the cohort as a whole.

**The dial, numerically** (arithmetic computed here from the formulas above):

| tau | eta_tau = arctanh(tau) | epsilon_tau = 2·arctanh(tau) | exp(epsilon_tau) = (1+tau)/(1−tau) | Max attacker accuracy (1+tau)/2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.0000 | 0.0000 | 1.0000 | 0.500 |
| 0.01 | 0.0100 | 0.0200 | 1.0202 | 0.505 |
| 0.05 | 0.0500 | 0.1001 | 1.1053 | 0.525 |
| 0.10 | 0.1003 | 0.2007 | 1.2222 | 0.550 |
| 0.25 | 0.2554 | 0.5108 | 1.6667 | 0.625 |
| 0.50 | 0.5493 | 1.0986 | 3.0000 | 0.750 |
| 0.90 | 1.4722 | 2.9444 | 19.000 | 0.950 |

**Relation to local differential privacy.** *Differential privacy* (DP) is the standard framework in which a mechanism's output distribution is required to change by at most a factor exp(epsilon) when the input changes. *Local* DP (LDP) is the variant where the randomization is applied by each individual to their own data before it ever leaves their hands — which is exactly our situation: one target, one path, one randomized release, no trusted curator. The pointwise ratio bound in Theorem 1 *is* an epsilon_tau-LDP guarantee over the admissible input-path domain. It is strictly stronger than the TV statement (pointwise ratio bounds imply TV bounds, but not conversely). The proposal nevertheless leads with tau rather than epsilon_tau because tau has the direct operational reading "attacker accuracy is at most (1 + tau)/2," whereas an epsilon is notoriously hard for non-specialists to interpret. Remember the collision: this epsilon_tau has nothing to do with PanMixer's per-block privacy risk eps_j or its operating point eps_private = 0.002.


The proof is short enough to test step by step against numbers, and the four questions below do that in two pairs. The first pair checks the bookkeeping: Step 2's interval on a worked example, and where the factor of 2 in epsilon_tau comes from.

> **Question** — *"Step 2 says Z_p is a weighted average of the bonus factors. Show me Z_p computed on the §4.10 worked example — T = 3, K = 2, tau = 0.5 — and show it landing inside [1, 1.7321]."*
>
> The numbers are in §4.10 and belong here too, because a proof step is easier to believe instantiated where it is stated. On that example, log Z_p = log(1.1005) + log(1.1019) + log(1.0902) = 0.2792, so **Z_p = 1.322** (derived), and the interval the proof promises is [1, exp(eta_tau)] = **[1, 1.7321]** (derived). 1.322 sits inside it.
>
> Two things to take from that rather than one. First, Z_p is a weighted average of factors each lying in [1, exp(eta_tau)], so it cannot escape that interval — that is the whole content of Step 2. Second, the interval is **the cheapest correctness assertion in the whole implementation**: log Z_p falls out of the forward pass for free as the sum of the per-block normalisers, so asserting 0 ≤ log Z_p ≤ eta_tau on every draw converts a proof step into a runtime check. `docs/terms.md` puts it in four words: *"Assert it, do not report it."* It is exit gate 1 (§4.16.5).

> **Question** — *"Where does the 2 in epsilon_tau = 2·arctanh(tau) come from? Is it the two utilities in the difference, or the two Z's in the ratio?"*
>
> Both, one factor each, and that is exactly why it is 2 and not 1.
>
> Write the likelihood ratio: Q_p(y)/Q_q(y) = exp(eta_tau·[u(p,y) − u(q,y)]) · (Z_q/Z_p).
>
> The **utility difference** lies in [−1, 1] because both utilities lie in [0, 1], so the first factor is at most exp(eta_tau). The **Z ratio** is at most exp(eta_tau)/1 = exp(eta_tau), because Step 2 puts every Z in [1, exp(eta_tau)]. Multiply: the ratio is at most exp(2·eta_tau), so the pointwise bound is s = 2·eta_tau = epsilon_tau (derived).
>
> This is also the single most dangerous formula in the project to mistype. **Never write epsilon_tau = arctanh(tau)** — that halves the stated budget and silently understates the leakage; the docs contract test checks the relation for exactly that reason. And the neighbouring error is worse still: using arctanh(tau) with an **unnormalized** utility. If u spans Δu rather than [0, 1], the correct calibration is arctanh(tau)/Δu, and getting it wrong breaks the theorem with no visible symptom.

The second pair checks the two results this section used without deriving them: the likelihood-ratio-to-TV conversion of Step 5, and the convexity inequality behind Corollary 1.

> **Question** — *"Step 5 says 'this is the standard step'. Can you prove the likelihood-ratio-to-TV inequality rather than cite it? I want to see why it's tanh and not something else."*
>
> Two lines, and it is worth doing rather than citing.
>
> Suppose for every outcome y you have Q_p(y) ≤ e^s·Q_q(y) and Q_q(y) ≤ e^s·Q_p(y), with s = 2·eta_tau. Let A be the event on which Q_p exceeds Q_q, and write a = Q_p(A), b = Q_q(A). Total variation distance is a − b. The two pointwise bounds give a ≤ e^s·b on A, and, applied to the complement, 1 − b ≤ e^s·(1 − a).
>
> Maximise a − b subject to both, which happens with both tight: a = e^s·b and 1 − b = e^s·(1 − a). Substituting the first into the second gives e^(2s)·b = e^s − 1 + b, so b = 1/(e^s + 1) and a = e^s/(e^s + 1). Therefore
>
> **TV ≤ (e^s − 1)/(e^s + 1) = tanh(s/2) = tanh(eta_tau) = tau** (derived).
>
> That is where the tanh comes from: it is not an approximation or a convention, it is the exact solution of the two-constraint maximisation — which is why arctanh inverts it exactly and why tau is attained rather than merely bounded.

> **Question** — *"Corollary 1 uses convexity of TV. Work it on the three-outcome micro-example from §4.1 with two cohort members and equal priors."*
>
> Take §4.1's distributions: P = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2) and Q = (0.4, 0.4, 0.2), with TV(P, Q) = 0.1. Let the attacker face a mixture of two cohort members with priors (0.5, 0.5), one contributing P and one contributing Q: the mixture is M = (0.45, 0.35, 0.2) (derived).
>
> Then TV(P, M) = ½·(|0.5 − 0.45| + |0.3 − 0.35| + 0) = ½·(0.05 + 0.05) = **0.05** (derived), and convexity gives the bound TV(P, M) ≤ 0.5·TV(P, P) + 0.5·TV(P, Q) = 0.5·0 + 0.5·0.1 = **0.05** (derived). Bound and value coincide here, which is the cleanest possible instance: mixing over cohort members cannot make the attacker's job **easier** than the worst pairwise case, which is what the corollary is for.

### 4.8 The conditions, and what breaks if each fails

The proposal's Section 2.5 lists **three** conditions that the theorem's proof actually depends on. There is a fourth requirement that the proposal states elsewhere as practical guidance (in its Section 2.6 on sampling versus Viterbi, and its Section 2.8 on repeated releases) rather than in the numbered list. We keep it as row 4 and label it as ours, because an implementer who ignores it loses the guarantee just as surely.

| # | Condition | What it means concretely | What breaks if violated |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Common support** (proposal condition 1) | Y_D fixed before looking at the target; no target-specific candidate lists | A candidate present for input p and absent for input q gives disjoint supports on that outcome; the likelihood ratio is infinite and TV jumps to 1 |
| 2 | **Target-independent baseline** (proposal condition 2) | R_D learned only from G and D | R_D(y) no longer cancels in Step 3 of the proof; there is no bound on the ratio at all |
| 3 | **Bounded utility** (proposal condition 3) | 0 ≤ u(p, y) ≤ 1 | Step 1 fails. If the raw utility has range Δu = u_max − u_min, the proposal's fix is to recalibrate: eta_tau = arctanh(tau)/Δu. If the range is *unbounded*, no calibration exists and there is no guarantee |
| 4 | **One release per genome, and sample rather than maximize** (our framing of the proposal's Section 2.6 and Section 2.8 guidance) | Do not independently resample the same genome for every query; never take the argmax | Two independent releases of the same genome give an attacker two draws. TV does not compose for free: the standard subadditivity bound for k independent releases is k·tau, which degrades fast and is only an upper bound, not a promise. Taking the argmax makes the mechanism deterministic, so TV becomes 0 or 1 and the guarantee is vacuous |

Condition 4 has a practical implication the proposal states explicitly: a deployed system should **sample one sanitized path and cache it**, serving that same path for every subsequent query about that genome.

Two further caveats from the proposal, which are limitations rather than conditions. First, the guarantee applies **only to the released sanitized path**; it says nothing about the raw genome data, which must be protected by other means. Second, the utility function must be chosen *before* inspecting target-specific attack outcomes — tuning phi_t against measured attack success on a real target would make the mechanism target-dependent through the back door. The proposal also notes that any target-dependent pruning of the HMM state space would require its own separate privacy analysis.


> **Question** — *"Condition 1 forbids target-specific candidate lists. Does that rule out any pruning of the 88 states — even pruning that's obviously fine, like dropping donors with no data in the region?"*
>
> No, and the line is sharp: pruning that is **target-independent** is allowed, because it redefines Y_D before the target is ever seen; pruning that is **target-dependent** is forbidden, and the proposal says such a state space would need its own separate privacy analysis.
>
> Now notice where that line falls, because this is the point at which the missing-data policy becomes a privacy decision rather than only a modelling one. The **RENORMALISE** policy of §4.17 — the one adopted — drops a donor from the state distribution at positions where that donor has no call. That is a per-position change to the state set — and it is target-independent, because whether donor j has a call at position t is a fact about donor j. So RENORMALISE is **allowed**. It would stop being allowed the moment the pruning rule consulted the target's own alleles.
>
> Two costs come with it anyway. Computationally, changing the per-position state set disturbs the stay/switch collapse that gives O(K) per step, because the transition matrix is no longer one value on the diagonal and one value everywhere else (§4.10.2, and §4.17's last **Question** works the interaction). And statistically, RENORMALISE is correct for a **not-applicable** missing call — the DNA does not exist on that chromosome — and wrong for an **assembly gap**, where the donor almost certainly has an allele we merely do not know, so it penalises poorly-assembled donors for reasons unrelated to genetics. That second cost is accepted rather than solved: it is why splitting the policy by cause stays on the table behind the `REASON` array, and why §4.17 states the bias alongside the choice.

> **Question** — *"Condition 4 says one release per genome. What if the same person is a target in two different studies, or two labs each run this on the same person? Nobody coordinates that. Is the guarantee just gone in practice?"*
>
> The honest answer is that composition is an **open question** and the operational control is not enforceable across institutions.
>
> What we have: for k releases of one genome the subadditive bound gives at most k·tau, and it is an **upper bound only** — loose, and it degrades linearly, so ten uncoordinated releases at tau = 0.10 bound nothing useful. The caching requirement — compute the release once and serve the same one forever — is the control, and it works inside one pipeline and not between two labs.
>
> So state it as a limitation rather than a solved problem: **one release per genome is an operational rule, not a theorem** (project status, `docs/plan.md`). It is also why the head-to-head's frontier is explicitly an evaluation device only — a frontier of k operating points is k correlated releases of the same genome, which is exactly the situation condition 4 forbids in deployment, and the attacks are therefore run per operating point and never pooled.

> **Question** — *"Condition 3's fix is eta_tau = arctanh(tau)/Δu. For our beta/phi construction, what is Δu — is it always exactly 1, or can a real block set make it smaller? And row 4 is labelled 'our framing' rather than one of the proposal's three. Is it a real condition, or a best practice?"*
>
> On Δu: it is **exactly 1 by construction**, and only by construction. With every phi_t in [0, 1] and the beta_t summing to 1, u = sum_t beta_t·phi_t attains 0 when nothing matches and 1 when everything matches, so the span is 1 and eta_tau = arctanh(tau) needs no division (derived). The warning that goes with it: **any change to phi_t reopens this.** Swap in a similarity that can exceed 1, or weights that do not sum to 1, and the calibration is silently wrong — which is the highest-severity error class recorded in this project, because the mechanism still runs and still produces plausible output.
>
> On row 4: it is a real condition on the **deployment**, not on the theorem. Theorem 1 as stated is about **one** release, so an implementer who ignores row 4 does not falsify the theorem — they make it **inapplicable to what they actually published**. That distinction is worth keeping in the table's wording, because "the theorem still holds" and "the theorem covers your artefact" are different claims and only the second one matters to a reader of the release.

### 4.9 Implementation: the baseline as a hidden Markov model

We cannot enumerate Y_D — the number of cohort-supported paths through a chromosome is astronomically large. So we need R_D and the tilt to have a structure that permits exact sampling without enumeration. Hidden Markov models provide exactly that.

A **hidden Markov model (HMM)** has a sequence of unobserved ("hidden") states, each depending only on the previous one, and each emitting an observation. Here:

- The path is cut into **T** ordered blocks, t = 1, …, T.
- At block t the hidden state **Z_t** takes one of **K** values, indexing which cohort haplotype (or which cohort-supported local traversal) the model is copying at that block.
- **h_{t,j}** is the actual local sequence/traversal that state j supplies at block t.
- **rho(j)** is the probability of starting in state j; **A_t(i, j)** is the probability of moving from state i at block t−1 to state j at block t. Transitions with probability zero encode graph-incompatible concatenations — pairs of local traversals that cannot be glued together because the graph has no edge joining them.

The baseline is then R_D(z_1..z_T) = rho(z_1) · product over t = 2..T of A_t(z_{t−1}, z_t). A state sequence z_1..z_T induces a released path by concatenation:

**Phi(z_1..z_T) = h_{1,z_1} ‖ h_{2,z_2} ‖ … ‖ h_{T,z_T}**,

where **‖ means concatenation** — glue the block sequences end to end, in order, to get one long sequence.

This is precisely the Li–Stephens "copying" model that Section 3 described: the released chromosome is a **mosaic** that copies one cohort haplotype for a stretch, occasionally switches to another (a modelled recombination), copies that one for a stretch, and so on. The whole model is built from the cohort and does not mention the target.

Now the key algebraic fact. Because u is **additive across blocks**, the exponential tilt factorizes — a sum inside an exponential becomes a product of exponentials:

exp(eta_tau · u(p, z_1..z_T)) = exp(eta_tau · sum_t beta_t · phi_t(p_t, h_{t,z_t})) = product over t of **psi_t(z_t)**, where **psi_t(j) = exp(eta_tau · beta_t · phi_t(p_t, h_{t,j}))**.

So the tilted distribution is

Q_p(z_1..z_T) proportional to rho(z_1)·psi_1(z_1) · product over t = 2..T of A_t(z_{t−1}, z_t)·psi_t(z_t).

**This is still an HMM.** The transitions are unchanged public cohort transitions; the private target enters *only* through the per-block local potentials psi_t, which are bounded by construction: 1 ≤ psi_t(j) ≤ exp(eta_tau · beta_t). In HMM language, psi_t plays exactly the role an emission probability plays — the target's block is the "observation" and psi_t measures how compatible each state is with it. So we can sample from Q_p with standard HMM machinery.


Each symbol in that construction hides a choice that this project has made, deferred or reserved, and the four questions below take them in turn. The first two are about the chain's skeleton: what counts as a block, and where the forbidden transitions come from.

> **Question** — *"T again. Here T is 'the number of ordered blocks'. Is T the PLINK block count, the number of chain positions inside one block, or the number of variants? This is the T question and I haven't settled it."*
>
> Three readings, side by side, and then what v1 does — which is now decided.
>
> | candidate for T | value on chr21 | what it buys | what it costs |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | PLINK LD block entries (PanMixer's unit) | **100,757** (measured in this project) | the same segmentation PanMixer uses, which makes a parity run easier to read | boundaries come from an external panel in base-pair coordinates, know nothing about graph structure, and are **not disjoint** — 9.2% of records sit inside an earlier record's span, 15.4% of blocks are affected (measured in this project). It also drags an external panel into the mechanism's input set, which nothing else in the design needs |
> | snarl-tree levels | the 257 kb region becomes **1** position instead of **370** (measured in this project) | positions disjoint by construction; every boundary is a real graph cut point, so switching donors at a boundary is graph-valid | not implemented, and it is the least explored of the three |
> | one variant per position | **340,824** (measured in this project) | maximal resolution, no external panel, no block bookkeeping, and the correct Li–Stephens model | largest T, thinnest per-position budget |
>
> What **v1** uses, decided 2026-09-28: the third row. **Model C — one chain over the whole chromosome, with the individual variant as the chain unit**, so T = **340,824** on chr21 before the coordinate cut (§4.16). There is now only **one** "T-like" number, which is the main simplification the decision buys: beta_v sums to 1 over the chain positions themselves, and there is no outer unit and no per-block normaliser. Model B — one chain whose units are LD blocks — is retained as a **fallback** if closer parity with PanMixer is wanted; model A, independent per-block chains with no inter-block transitions, is **ruled out**.
>
> What that decision does **not** close: the tandem-duplication sub-problem — **a path containing a tandem duplication visits a position twice**, so "the allele of path p at position t" is not well-defined for such a path, which both u_path and phi_v assume it is. That must be settled before the sampler is written, because it decides what a position is at those sites (§4.16.7).

> **Question** — *"'Transitions with probability zero encode graph-incompatible concatenations.' How do we find those? Do we need the snarl tree, and is that the same thing you meant by 'T should come from the snarl tree rather than from PLINK intervals'?"*
>
> It is the same structural object, yes — the snarl tree is the graph's own nested decomposition of bubbles, the thing `vg deconstruct -a` walks to emit `LV` and `PS` tags — and its appeal for both purposes is the same: a snarl boundary is a genuine cut point in the graph, so switching donors there is **graph-valid by construction**, and the segmentation it induces is disjoint.
>
> For **v1** we do not need it. v1 copies whole donor alleles at existing VCF rows, so every allele it emits is one a real donor carries at that row; there is no candidate concatenation that no donor traverses at the level of a single row. What v1 does **not** establish is that the assembled row vector is jointly a single walk through the graph — and neither does PanMixer, whose stacker writes one allele index per record with **no sequence assembly step and no coherence check** (measured in this project). Whether Y_D can be restricted to graph-valid paths is recorded as an open question, and the snarl tree is the leading route to it (§4.16.3, second **Caution**).

The other two are about what sits on that skeleton: what a state actually supplies at a block in v1, and whether the tilt still factorises inside a block.

> **Question** — *"h_{t,j} is 'the actual local sequence that state j supplies'. For v1, at a block with three variants, what is h_{t,j} concretely — three allele indices? Show me the array."*
>
> Three allele indices, yes — and it is worth being fully concrete because the state-semantics error has recurred twice in this project.
>
> A state j is a **donor haplotype**, one of K = **88** — the full cohort, because our target is external and nothing is held out (K = 86 arises only in the optional head-to-head arm, where a cohort member stands in as target; see §4.16.1). The data is `X[i, s, h]` from Step 9: `i` indexes the 44 samples, `s` the 340,824 chr21 records, `h` the two strands, and the entry is an **allele index** — 0 for REF, 1, 2, 3… for the nth ALT **as listed in that row**, and `-1` for missing. A donor haplotype is a pair `(i, h)`.
>
> Under **model C** — one chain over the chromosome, one position per variant — a state supplies exactly **one** allele index at each position: h_{v,j} = `X[i, v, h]`. The three-index array in the question is what a *block-unit* chain (model B, the fallback) would supply at one step: at a block whose three variants are rows `s1, s2, s3`, the length-3 vector `[X[i, s1, h], X[i, s2, h], X[i, s3, h]]` for that donor — for example `[0, 2, 0]`, meaning "reference at the first variant, second ALT at the second, reference at the third". The real worked instance is block 1077: five variants at rows 34816–34820, where the target carries `[0,0,0,0,0]` and **82 of 88** donor haplotypes carry exactly that same vector (measured in this project). Under C that block is five consecutive chain positions rather than one step, and the same five numbers are read one at a time. Either way h_{t,j} is not a sequence and not a variant; it is what one donor carries at the rows the step covers.

> **Question** — *"The factorisation says exp of a sum becomes a product of psi_t. That works because u is additive across blocks. Is our phi_t additive across positions inside a block too? If not, can FFBS still sample it exactly?"*
>
> This is the constraint §4.16.4 exists to state: **phi_t must decompose along the chain positions inside a block.** A whole-block similarity that does not decompose **cannot be sampled exactly by FFBS** (project constraint, `docs/plan.md`).
>
> The reason is mechanical. FFBS is exact because the tilted object is still an HMM, and it is still an HMM because the tilt enters as a **per-position potential** psi_t(j). If phi_t is a function of the whole block that cannot be written as a sum over its positions, the tilt cannot be pushed inside the chain, the forward recursion no longer has the right factors, and what comes out of the backward pass is not a draw from Q_p.
>
> Under **model C** the constraint is satisfied trivially, because a chain position **is** a single variant and there is nothing inside it to decompose. The utility collapses to its simplest form: `phi_v` is an **indicator** — 1 if the drawn donor carries the target's allele at v and 0 otherwise — and `beta_v` = w_v / Σ_u w_u with w_v = 1/support(v). u = Σ_v beta_v · phi_v is then a weighted average of indicators, bounded in [0, 1] and additive by construction, with no per-block normaliser anywhere. The k-mer weighted Jaccard of §4.5 is the illustration that most obviously does **not** decompose, which is why it is deferred to post-release reporting rather than used as a potential (§4.16.4). Any future phi has to pass this test before it is a candidate at all — and under the model-B fallback, where a step covers a whole block, it is a real constraint rather than an automatic one.

### 4.10 Forward-filtering / backward-sampling (FFBS), from scratch

We need to draw a *whole state sequence* Z_1..Z_T from the tilted distribution, exactly — not approximately, and not the most likely one. FFBS does this in two passes.

**Pass 1: forward filtering (left to right).** Maintain, for each block t, a length-K vector alpha_t where alpha_t(j) is the probability that the chain is in state j at block t *given everything seen up to and including block t*.

- Initialize: r_1(j) = rho(j)·psi_1(j); c_1 = sum_j r_1(j); alpha_1(j) = r_1(j)/c_1.
- For t = 2..T: r_t(j) = psi_t(j) · sum_i alpha_{t−1}(i)·A_t(i, j); c_t = sum_j r_t(j); alpha_t(j) = r_t(j)/c_t.

*In words:* to get the unnormalized weight of state j at block t, first ask "how likely is it that the chain arrives in state j at all?" (that is the sum over the previous state i of where we were times the probability of moving i → j), then multiply by how well state j suits the target at this block. The division by c_t rescales the vector so its entries sum to 1.

The per-block renormalization by c_t prevents numerical underflow (multiplying T ≈ 100,000 small numbers together would otherwise round to zero in floating point). As a free by-product, log Z_p = sum over t of log c_t.

**Pass 2: backward sampling (right to left).** Now actually draw a sequence.

- Sample Z_T from the **categorical** distribution alpha_T. (A categorical draw just means "pick one of K options with the listed probabilities.")
- For t = T−1 down to 1, given the already-sampled Z_{t+1} = j, sample Z_t from weights proportional to alpha_t(i)·A_{t+1}(i, j), normalized over i.

**Why this is exact.** The joint distribution factorizes as P(Z_T) · P(Z_{T−1} | Z_T) · P(Z_{T−2} | Z_{T−1}) · … — each backward step is the exact conditional of Z_t given Z_{t+1} and all the evidence, because in a Markov chain, conditioning on Z_{t+1} makes Z_t independent of everything to the right. So the sequence produced is an exact draw from Q_p. The forward pass computes the conditionals; the backward pass consumes them.

**Worked example.** T = 3 blocks, K = 2 states. Start rho = (0.5, 0.5). Stay/switch transitions with stay probability a = 0.9 and switch probability b = 0.1 (with K = 2 these sum to 1). Take **tau = 0.5**, so eta_tau = arctanh(0.5) = 0.5493, and equal block weights beta_t = 1/3, so eta_tau·beta_t = 0.1831. Suppose the target matches state 1 in blocks 1 and 2 and state 2 in block 3:

| Block t | phi_t(state 1) | phi_t(state 2) | psi_t(1) | psi_t(2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.0 | 0.0 | 1.2009 | 1.0000 |
| 2 | 0.8 | 0.2 | 1.1578 | 1.0373 |
| 3 | 0.0 | 1.0 | 1.0000 | 1.2009 |

Forward pass:

- t = 1: r_1 = (0.5·1.2009, 0.5·1.0) = (0.6005, 0.5000); c_1 = 1.1005; **alpha_1 = (0.5457, 0.4543)**.
- t = 2: prediction = 0.1·1 + 0.8·alpha_1 = (0.5365, 0.4635); r_2 = (1.1578·0.5365, 1.0373·0.4635) = (0.6212, 0.4807); c_2 = 1.1019; **alpha_2 = (0.5637, 0.4363)**.
- t = 3: prediction = 0.1 + 0.8·alpha_2 = (0.5510, 0.4490); r_3 = (1.0·0.5510, 1.2009·0.4490) = (0.5510, 0.5393); c_3 = 1.0902; **alpha_3 = (0.5054, 0.4946)**.

(The "prediction" line uses the stay/switch shortcut derived in §4.10.2: with two states, b·(alpha(1)+alpha(2)) + (a − b)·alpha(j) = 0.1·1 + 0.8·alpha(j).)

log Z_p = log(1.1005) + log(1.1019) + log(1.0902) = 0.2792, so Z_p = 1.322 — which sits inside the proof's interval [1, exp(eta_tau)] = [1, 1.7321], exactly as Step 2 of §4.7 promised.

Backward pass. Draw Z_3 from (0.5054, 0.4946); say we get **Z_3 = 2**. Then for t = 2 the weights are alpha_2(1)·A(1→2) = 0.5637·0.1 = 0.0564 and alpha_2(2)·A(2→2) = 0.4363·0.9 = 0.3926; normalized, **(0.126, 0.874)** — state 2 is strongly favoured, because switching is expensive. Say we draw **Z_2 = 2**. Then for t = 1 the weights are 0.5457·0.1 = 0.0546 and 0.4543·0.9 = 0.4089; normalized, **(0.118, 0.882)**.

Notice what the example teaches: at block 1 the tilt genuinely favours state 1 (alpha_1 puts 0.546 on it, above the 0.5 it would have with no tilt), but once the backward pass commits to state 2 at block 3, the stay probability drags the whole sequence toward state 2. The public transition model and the private tilt are genuinely competing, and at these settings the transition model usually wins. That is what "the target may only bend the baseline a little" looks like in practice.


> **Question** — *"In your worked example the transition model beats the tilt — you say so yourself. Is that because tau = 0.5, because T = 3, or because a = 0.9? Re-run it with a = 0.5 so I can see the tilt win once."*
>
> It is `a`. Re-run the identical example with stay probability a = 0.5 and switch b = 0.5 (with K = 2 they must sum to 1), keeping tau = 0.5, eta_tau·beta_t = 0.1831 and the same psi_t table. The stay/switch shortcut becomes b·1 + (a − b)·alpha(j) = 0.5 + 0·alpha(j), i.e. the prediction is **(0.5, 0.5) at every step**: a uniform transition matrix forgets the past completely, so alpha_t is driven by psi_t alone.
>
> - t = 1: r = (0.6005, 0.5000); c = **1.1005**; alpha = **(0.5456, 0.4544)** — the same alpha_1 as the original example, because alpha_1 does not depend on the transitions at all (§4.10 rounds it to 0.5457 / 0.4543; the exact value is 0.60045/1.10045 = 0.54564)
> - t = 2: r = (0.5789, 0.5187); c = **1.0976**; alpha = **(0.5274, 0.4726)**
> - t = 3: r = (0.5000, 0.6005); c = **1.1005**; alpha = **(0.4544, 0.5456)**
>
> log Z_p = 0.2845, so Z_p = 1.329, inside [1, 1.7321] as before (all derived).
>
> Now the backward pass, which is where the difference shows. Draw Z_3 from (0.4544, 0.5456) and say it comes out 2. The weights for t = 2 are alpha_2(1)·A(1→2) = 0.5274·0.5 = 0.2637 and alpha_2(2)·A(2→2) = 0.4726·0.5 = 0.2363, which normalise to **(0.5274, 0.4726)** — identical to alpha_2. The future has told you **nothing**, because the transitions are uniform, so Z_2 is drawn from the tilt alone and state 1 — the state the target matches at block 2 — is favoured at 0.527 against 0.5.
>
> Compare with the original example at a = 0.9, where the same weights came out **(0.126, 0.874)**: there the commitment at block 3 dragged the whole sequence to state 2 and the tilt lost. So the competition is between the **stay probability** and the **tilt**, and at realistic separations in real data the stay probability is the larger force by a wide margin — which is the same thing §4.10.4 says in budget terms.

> **Question** — *"log Z_p = sum of log c_t comes out for free, and the proof says Z_p is in [1, exp(eta_tau)]. So that's an assertion I can put in the code that fails loudly if the calibration is wrong. Is that what exit gate 1 means?"*
>
> Yes — and it is the cheapest correctness assertion in the whole implementation, so it should be an assert and not a logged metric.
>
> Exit gate 1 of the v1 specification requires four things together: the sampler matches **exact enumeration** on a toy model; exact TV ≤ tau across a grid of tau values and many input pairs; **log Z_p inside [0, eta_tau] on every draw**; and tau = 0 reproducing the baseline model exactly (§4.16.5).
>
> Why each of those is needed rather than just the Z_p check: log Z_p in range confirms the **tilt** is calibrated, exact enumeration confirms the **sampler** draws what it claims, the TV grid confirms the **bound** empirically at a scale where it can be computed exactly, and tau = 0 confirms there is no accidental target dependence anywhere in the path. A calibration bug that passes the Z_p check can still fail the enumeration check, and a sampler bug that passes enumeration can still fail at tau = 0 if some artefact was built with the target in it — which, per §4.4, is currently true of our frequency table.

> **Question** — *"The backward step conditions on Z_{t+1} only. But the released path is the concatenation, and two different state sequences can give the same path. Does that break the 'exact draw' claim? And what do we actually store at the end — a state sequence or a path?"*
>
> It does not break it. The draw is exact **over state sequences**, and the released path is a deterministic function of the state sequence. Two state sequences mapping to the same path merely merge outcomes, and **deterministic post-processing can never increase TV** (§4.10.1) — so collisions can only help, never hurt, and the bound carries through to the path.
>
> What is stored is the **path** — one sanitized path for one external individual, which is the deliverable. Optionally it can also be written in the shape PanMixer's evaluators eat: `new_haplotypes.npy`, shape `(n_sites, 2)`, int16, `-1` for missing, **row-aligned to the chromosome's VCF**. That emission is **comparison-arm only**: it is the cheapest way to run PanMixer's *attack* evaluators on our release unmodified and is worth doing for the head-to-head, but it is **not a requirement of the mechanism and not an exit gate** (project definition, `docs/terms.md`, `new_haplotypes`; see §4.16.1).
>
> One hazard that comes with that seam and deserves an assertion: it is **positional**. A row-order mismatch does not error, it silently scores the wrong variants (project note, `docs/terms.md`). Keeping the state sequence as a debugging artefact is fine and target-independent; what must never happen is the evaluation consuming a file whose row order was not checked.

#### 4.10.1 Why sampling, not argmax

It is tempting to run **Viterbi** instead of sampling. Viterbi is the standard HMM dynamic-programming algorithm that returns the single most probable state sequence — an **argmax**, meaning the one highest-scoring answer, returned deterministically, with no randomness at all. **Using it here would destroy the guarantee.** A deterministic mechanism has a **point-mass** output distribution — probability 1 on one outcome and 0 on everything else. For two inputs whose argmaxes differ, the two point masses sit on different outcomes, so their supports are disjoint and TV(Q_p, Q_q) = 1: the worst possible value, and an attacker accuracy bound of (1 + 1)/2 = 1, i.e. no protection. Randomness is not a nuisance to be minimized here; it *is* the privacy. The proposal states this explicitly when it contrasts forward sampling with Viterbi decoding.

One more piece of good news: the released path is p̃ = Phi(Z_1..Z_T), a deterministic function of the sampled states. **Deterministic post-processing can never increase TV** — if you could raise the distance by relabelling outcomes, you could build a better test on the relabelled outcomes and beat the optimal test on the originals, a contradiction. So TV(Law(p̃ | p), Law(p̃ | q)) ≤ TV(Q_p, Q_q) ≤ tau. The guarantee survives the conversion from states to an actual path.


> **Question** — *"Viterbi gives TV = 1. But sampling from a very peaked distribution is almost Viterbi. At what point does a peaked sampler become effectively deterministic — is there a tau where this argument stops protecting us?"*
>
> There is no cliff, because peakedness is exactly what tau controls. The bound is proved for **every** tau < 1, and the maximum ratio between any two candidates' bonus factors is exp(eta_tau) — **1.7321** at tau = 0.5 and **4.3589** at tau = 0.9, since eta_tau = arctanh(tau) is 0.549306 and 1.472219 respectively (all derived, and the eta_tau values match §4.7's dial table). A peaked sampler is a sampler at large tau, and its attacker ceiling is (1 + tau)/2, which is **0.95** at tau = 0.9 (derived): the guarantee has not stopped applying, it has stopped being worth much. As tau → 1 the bound degenerates to no guarantee at all, which is the same statement from the other side.
>
> The real-data version of this question is the opposite worry, and it is the one that has actually bitten: PanMixer's shipped sampler **looked** random and was effectively deterministic, returning a block identical to a single donor haplotype in **300 of 300** real chr21 HMM-path blocks (measured in this project). That failure was not a tau too large; it was transition constants that put switch mass at **5.8 × 10⁻¹²** (measured in this project). A mechanism can be effectively deterministic for reasons that have nothing to do with the privacy parameter, which is why exit gate 3 tests the sampler's output directly rather than trusting the parameter (§4.16.5).

> **Question** — *"Does this rule out any use of argmax anywhere — for example picking the best beta_t allocation, or picking a start state?"*
>
> No. The forbidden thing is an argmax over a quantity that depends on the **target**. A deterministic function of the private input has a point-mass output distribution, and two inputs with different argmaxes then have disjoint supports and TV = 1.
>
> Design choices made **before the target is seen** are fine, however they were arrived at: choosing a beta_t allocation from a public fixed rule, picking a start-state distribution rho, choosing k for a k-mer similarity, selecting a missing-data policy. All of those are target-independent and may be optimised however you like — they are part of defining R_D and Y_D, which conditions 1 and 2 require to be fixed before the target arrives.
>
> One retired idea is worth naming here so it does not come back: **MAP/Viterbi decoding of the tilted HMM was retired in this project on 2026-08-29, before use**, because it is not a draw from the release distribution and carries **no** privacy guarantee while producing output indistinguishable in appearance from a valid release. It is the natural thing to reach for in an HMM codebase and its failure is silent. Do not re-add it.

#### 4.10.2 Cost

With a **dense** K-by-K transition matrix, the forward recursion costs O(K²) per block (each of K new states sums over K old states), hence O(T·K²) overall; backward sampling costs O(K) per block, hence O(T·K); computing the potentials costs O(T·K). The dense forward pass dominates.

But PanMixer's transition model is **stay/switch**: A_t(i, j) = a_t when i = j and b_t when i ≠ j — one probability for staying on the current donor haplotype and one for switching to any other. That structure collapses the sum. Writing S_{t−1} = sum_i alpha_{t−1}(i) for the total of the previous forward vector,

r_t(j) = psi_t(j)·[b_t·S_{t−1} + (a_t − b_t)·alpha_{t−1}(j)].

*Where that comes from:* every previous state contributes b_t to arriving at j, except state j itself which contributes a_t. So add b_t times everything, then correct the single diagonal term by (a_t − b_t). One shared scalar plus one per-state correction gives all K forward values in **O(K)** per block, so **O(T·K)** for the whole forward pass. The backward weights collapse identically: given a sampled next state j, the weight is a_{t+1}·alpha_t(j) for i = j and b_{t+1}·alpha_t(i) otherwise. No dense matrix is ever formed. **Total: O(T·K) time.** The proposal also notes a middle case: if only a sparse edge set E_t of state pairs can legally be concatenated at block t, the forward pass costs O(|E_t|) there, for a total of O(T·K + sum_t |E_t|).

Memory: storing every alpha_t is O(T·K).

**Our numbers on chr21.** On this project's rebuilt chr21 the HPRC graph deconstructs to **340,824 VCF records** and the state set is **K = 88 haplotypes** (44 individuals × 2 — the full cohort, because our target is external and nothing is held out). **Under the decided chain architecture, model C, T = 340,824**: one chain over the chromosome stepping variant to variant, so T·K ≈ **30.0 million** operations per haplotype and the stored forward vectors are 340,824 × 88 × 8 bytes ≈ **240 MB** in float64. The figures worked through immediately below use the **block-unit** count instead — the block dictionary has **100,757 entries**, giving T·K ≈ 8.87 million operations and ≈ 71 MB of alpha — because that is the model-B fallback and the unit PanMixer works in; both counts are measured in this project and recorded in `docs/memory.md` under 2026-09-18, with the run logged in `logs/pm_chr21.log`. Name which T any figure used. Either way it is cheap. For comparison, our measured PanMixer run takes **68 s and 1.7 GB peak resident memory per subject-chromosome on chr21** (measured in this project; `docs/memory.md` 2026-09-18, raw figures in `logs/pm_obf.log`), so the sampler itself will not be the bottleneck. The proposal separately notes that for a diploid cohort of n = 500 individuals, K would be at most about 2n = 1,000 states before de-duplication — still cheap.


> **Question** — *"K = 88 because the cohort is 44 people. What if we used the 3,202 panel haplotypes as donors instead? Does a bigger donor set help privacy, help utility, or neither?"*
>
> Utility, not privacy — and it would reintroduce a problem we already have.
>
> Privacy: the bound does not mention K anywhere. tau is a statement about the ratio of two release distributions, and enlarging the state space changes both distributions without changing the bound. So a bigger donor panel buys **nothing** on the privacy axis.
>
> Utility: it enlarges Y_D, which makes a plausible output easier to find — with more donors there is more chance that some mosaic closely resembles the target, so u(p, y) can reach higher values. Cost is linear in K for the stay/switch forward pass: chr21 at T = 100,757 and K = 88 is about **8.87 million** operations per haplotype and about **71 MB** of stored alpha columns at 64-bit (both derived), so a panel of 6,404 haplotypes would be roughly 73× both figures — still not a compute problem, but no longer a trivial memory one.
>
> The catch: the 30x panel **contains 39 of the 44 HPRC donors, including the target** (measured in this project). Using panel haplotypes as donors would put the target's own haplotypes into the donor set, which is the condition-2 problem of §4.4 in its most direct form. A bigger donor panel is only usable if it is demonstrably target-free.

> **Question** — *"8.87 million operations and 71 MB of alphas. Is that per haplotype, per chromosome, or per genome? And if it's per chromosome, what's chr1?"*
>
> **Per haplotype, per chromosome, and the chromosome is chr21** — the only chromosome rebuilt in this project. The figures are T = 100,757 blocks × K = 88 states ≈ **8.87 million** operations, and 100,757 × 88 × 8 bytes ≈ **71 MB** of stored forward columns (both derived from measured T and K). A chain that steps once per **variant** instead, which is what v1 does, is **30.0 million** operations and ≈ **240 MB** of alpha over the whole chromosome (both derived; §4.16.6 collects the arithmetic and §4.13 explains why 240 MB is not a peak-memory figure). Against PanMixer's measured **68 s / 1.7 GB** for one subject-chromosome (measured in this project), compute is not a constraint.
>
> **The 22-chromosome figures have not been computed in this project**, so there is no genome-scale number to quote and none should be invented. What can be said is the scaling shape: both cost and memory are linear in T at fixed K, and chr21 is the smallest autosome, so a genome-wide chain would be roughly the sum of 22 such numbers with T set by whatever the chosen definition of a chain position turns out to be — which is the reserved T decision again (§4.16.8, item 1), and the reason the genome-scale number has not been quoted rather than merely not computed.

> **Question** — *"The stay/switch collapse needs A_t(i, j) to be one value for all i ≠ j. If we ever want donor-specific transition rates — say downweighting a poorly assembled donor — does the O(T·K) collapse survive?"*
>
> No, and it is worth knowing the price before wanting the feature. The collapse works because a stay/switch matrix has exactly two distinct entries, so the prediction step is one shared scalar plus a per-state correction: b·(sum_i alpha(i)) + (a − b)·alpha(j). Donor-specific rates give a matrix with K distinct rows, and the prediction step becomes a full matrix-vector product — **O(K²) per position**, or O(|E_t|) if the transition graph is sparse and you exploit that.
>
> At chr21 scale that is 100,757 × 88² ≈ **780 million** operations per haplotype against 8.87 million (derived) — about 88× more, which is affordable but no longer free.
>
> This connects directly to the adopted missing-data policy, which is why it belongs here rather than in a footnote: **RENORMALISE modifies the state distribution per position**, so it is exactly this kind of departure from a single off-diagonal value. Having chosen RENORMALISE we are therefore also choosing to recompute the running total at those positions rather than carry it forward — §4.17's last **Question** works through the version that survives — and to normalise transitions by the **live** donor count rather than a constant K. The plain O(K) collapse survives that; what does not survive is the assumption that the total can be reused. It should be written into the sampler deliberately.

#### 4.10.3 Diploid releases

Humans are **diploid**: every cell carries two copies of each autosome, one from each parent. To be precise about the counting, because it is easy to garble: a human karyotype has **23 chromosome pairs**, but there are **24 distinct chromosome sequences** (22 autosomes plus X plus Y). The often-quoted figure of about **3.1 billion base pairs is the haploid genome size** — one copy of each chromosome; a diploid cell contains roughly twice that much DNA. For our purposes the consequence is simply that the target supplies two paths, p = (p⁽¹⁾, p⁽²⁾), not one.

The proposal's safest formulation treats the pair as a single private input with joint utility

u_diploid(p, y) = [u(p⁽¹⁾, y⁽¹⁾) + u(p⁽²⁾, y⁽²⁾)]/2.

Averaging two numbers in [0, 1] stays in [0, 1], so condition 3 still holds and **the same tau guarantee covers the complete diploid release** — not tau per haplotype, tau for the pair. This matters: releasing two haplotypes each with its own tau budget would *not* give tau for the pair, for the same compositional reason that two independent releases of one genome do not (condition 4).


> **Question** — *"u_diploid averages the two haplotypes. So the sampler could hand back a near-perfect copy of haplotype 1 and garbage for haplotype 2 and score the same as something balanced. Is that a real failure mode, and does it leak more than a balanced release?"*
>
> It is a real **utility** failure mode and not a privacy one, and the two halves should be answered separately.
>
> Privacy: tau bounds the release of the **pair**, however the average was achieved. u_diploid(p, y) = (u(p⁽¹⁾, y⁽¹⁾) + u(p⁽²⁾, y⁽²⁾))/2 lies in [0, 1] whatever its two components are, and the proof uses nothing about how the average is composed. So an unbalanced release is not a leak. (The neighbouring error **is** fatal: using a **sum** instead of a mean leaves [0, 1] and **doubles the effective tilt**, and then the guarantee does not hold.)
>
> Utility: it is a genuine defect of the objective. A release that is faithful on one strand and useless on the other is worth much less to a downstream user than a release that is moderately faithful on both, and the objective cannot tell them apart. Whether it actually happens is **unmeasured** — no tau has been run end to end in this project, so there is no distribution of per-strand utilities to look at. It should be measured as soon as there is one, and the instrument already exists: `MIA_privacy.py` computes per-haplotype match fractions against the target's original haplotypes (measured in this project, reading the code), so a per-strand `target_fidelity` is available at no extra cost.

> **Question** — *"'Not tau per haplotype, tau for the pair.' If I ran the mechanism twice, once per haplotype, each at tau, what would the pair actually be — 2·tau?"*
>
> At most **2·tau**, by the subadditive composition bound with k = 2 — and "at most" is doing real work, because the bound is an **upper bound only** and the true value can be anything up to it. So two independent per-haplotype runs at tau = 0.10 give a pair bounded at 0.20, against a single diploid run at tau = 0.10 which bounds the pair at 0.10 (derived).
>
> That is the entire reason the diploid utility is defined as a mean over the pair rather than applied per strand: one calibration covering the whole diploid release is strictly stronger than two calibrations composed. It is the same arithmetic as condition 4 — repeated releases of one genome compose linearly and loosely — applied to the two strands of a single genome instead of to two separate publications.

> **Question** — *"Do the two haplotypes share a donor panel? Can haplotype 1 and haplotype 2 of the release copy the same donor at the same block, producing an implausibly homozygous release?"*
>
> They share the donor panel, and **whether the two draws are coupled has not been specified in this project** — so the honest answer is that nothing currently prevents both strands from copying the same donor at the same block, and no measurement of how often that would happen exists.
>
> The consequence would be a **plausibility** problem rather than a privacy one: a release that is homozygous across long stretches is unusual in a way a downstream analyst might notice. Note what would *not* catch it. `af_loss` and `ld_loss` measure the distortion a release inflicts on the **cohort's** allele frequencies and linkage, and our release never edits the cohort, so neither is defined for our release object and neither is a metric this project computes (`docs/terms.md`). The honest instrument here is an ensemble property of our own draws — the LD decay curve of R releases against the cohort's at real distances — which is a different quantity that has not been specified, let alone run. PanMixer's arm has a related pathology measured for a related reason: its sampled block equals a single donor's block in **300 of 300** cases (measured in this project), so its two strands are each a verbatim donor copy.
>
> Flag it as an unmeasured concern and a specification gap, not as a defect: it is a decision that has not been made yet, and it should be made explicitly when the sampler is written.

#### 4.10.4 An honest tension: the budget is thin

Here is arithmetic worth confronting early. The per-position tilt factor is at most exp(eta_tau · beta_t), and the beta_t must sum to 1 across all T positions. If we weight positions uniformly, beta_t = 1/T. The table below is computed at **T = 100,757** — chr21's block-dictionary count (measured in this project; `docs/memory.md`, 2026-09-18) — because that is the number the rest of Part A works in; under the decided model C the chain has **T = 340,824** positions and every entry gets about 3.4× smaller still, which sharpens the tension without changing its shape. ⚠ The **total** tilt exp(eta_tau) is **invariant in T**: more positions each move less and they compose to the same total. What T changes is how visible the tilt is at any one position.

| tau | eta_tau | Uniform per-block max tilt exp(eta_tau/T) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.10 | 0.1003 | 1.0000010 |
| 0.50 | 0.5493 | 1.0000055 |
| 0.90 | 1.4722 | 1.0000146 |

At that level the tilt is numerically indistinguishable from nothing: the output is, to six decimal places, a pure draw from R_D. Concentrating the weight helps — with beta spread uniformly over only m blocks and zero elsewhere, the per-block factor is exp(eta_tau/m), which at tau = 0.5 is 1.7321 for m = 1, 1.0565 for m = 10, and 1.0055 for m = 100 — but concentration means abandoning target-specific utility everywhere else.

This is not a flaw in the proof; it is the honest price of a **whole-chromosome worst-case** guarantee, which must hold even for the adversarially chosen pair of inputs that differ at every single block. Several **open questions** follow directly, and they are research questions for this project rather than settled facts:

- Should the guarantee be stated per chromosome, per region, or per some smaller unit, and what is the right composition story across units?
- Should beta_t be concentrated on a small set of clinically or analytically important blocks, and if so chosen how — noting that any *target-dependent* choice would violate condition 2 and void the theorem?
- What tau actually yields acceptable downstream utility (`target_fidelity`, read mapping, genotyping) on real data? **We have not measured this.** No tau has yet been run end to end in this project. Note that the list deliberately excludes `af_loss` and `ld_loss`: they measure distortion of the cohort's own statistics and are not defined for our release object (`docs/terms.md`).


This tension is the strongest objection to the whole design, so it gets five questions. The first two ask whether the mechanism is worth building at this scope, and how the budget may legally be concentrated.

> **Question** — *"exp(eta_tau/T) = 1.0000055 at tau = 0.5. That is nothing. So the mechanism at whole-chromosome scope is indistinguishable from publishing a random cohort path. Why are we building this? What is the version of this that actually works?"*
>
> Take the objection at full strength, because it is correct as arithmetic: with uniform weights on chr21's **T = 100,757** blocks, the largest per-block bonus factor at tau = 0.5 is exp(0.549306/100,757) = **1.0000055** (derived), which is indistinguishable from 1 in any float you would print. At whole-chromosome scope with uniform weights, the output **is** effectively a draw from R_D.
>
> Three escape routes exist, and which of them is right is an **open research question for this project**, not a solved design:
>
> 1. **Narrower scope units with a stated composition story.** Release per region rather than per chromosome, with beta_t summing to 1 within the unit and an explicit composition bound across units. The cost is that composition across units is the loose k·tau bound, and that a frontier of units is correlated releases.
> 2. **Concentrated beta_t.** Put the weight on m blocks instead of all T. The factor becomes exp(eta_tau/m), which is worth something for small m — the last **Question** of this subsection extends the table.
> 3. **A larger tau.** Honest, and it costs exactly what the dial says: tau = 0.9 bounds an equal-prior attacker at **0.95** (derived), which is close to no protection.
>
> What must not be said is that the tension is resolved. It is the honest price of a worst-case guarantee stated over a whole chromosome, and **no value of tau has been run end to end here**, so there is not even a measured utility number to weigh against it yet.

> **Question** — *"Concentrating beta on m blocks: chosen how? Any choice that looks at the target breaks condition 2. But 'clinically important blocks' is a fixed public list — is that allowed, and does it mean the guarantee only protects the blocks we decided to care about?"*
>
> Allowed, and no — and the second half is the part not to assume.
>
> Allowed: a **fixed public** allocation is target-independent, so it is part of defining the mechanism before the target arrives, exactly like choosing rho or k. A clinically curated block list, a list of exonic blocks, a list weighted by W_t computed from cohort support counts — all fine. What is forbidden is any allocation that consults the target's own path, which includes the tempting one: putting the weight where **this** target's material is rarest. That is PanMixer's `eps_j`-style reasoning, and using it inside our utility would be fatal, because `eps_j` is a function of the private input's own path.
>
> And no, concentrating beta does not concentrate privacy. beta_t allocates **utility importance** — which blocks the output tries hardest to resemble. tau bounds the distinguishability of the **whole released path** regardless of where beta sits, because the bound comes from u lying in [0, 1] and nothing else. So the blocks with beta_t = 0 are not unprotected; they are merely un-optimised, and their content is drawn from R_D alone.

The next two move from the theory to v1: whether the choice of chain unit escapes this arithmetic, and what v1 will measure first.

> **Question** — *"Does the choice of chain unit change this arithmetic? If each unit gets its own tau, the per-unit tilt is exp(arctanh(tau)) not exp(arctanh(tau)/T) — that's a completely different regime. Which one is v1?"*
>
> v1 is in the **exp(eta_tau/T)** regime, and no choice of unit escapes it.
>
> The reason is the condition that makes the utility bounded in the first place: the beta must sum to 1 across all T positions, so a larger T forces each position's share to be smaller. Under model C, T = **340,824** on chr21 and the per-position factor at tau = 0.5 is exp(0.549306/340,824) = **1.0000016** (derived). ⚠ But note what this does **not** mean: the **total** tilt exp(eta_tau) is **invariant in T**. More positions each move less and they compose to exactly the same total. Changing the unit redistributes the budget; it does not spend more or less of it.
>
> Giving every unit its own full tau would be a different mechanism with a different claim: T independent releases of T disjoint pieces of one genome, whose composition is the loose bound k·tau with k = T. At T = 340,824 that bounds nothing. So the "different regime" is real and it is not available for free.
>
> The **unit** is now settled — the individual variant, model C, decided 2026-09-28 on measured recombination rather than on this arithmetic (§4.16). What remains open is what a position means where a path visits the same node twice, at the hypervariable sites of §4.16.7, and how the budget should be allocated across positions at all (§4.16.8, item 5).

> **Question** — *"'We have not measured this. No tau has yet been run end to end.' What's the first number we'll get, and what value of it would tell us the method doesn't work?"*
>
> The first numbers come from **exit gate 1**, and they are all correctness numbers rather than utility numbers: the sampler matched against **exact enumeration** on a toy model; **exact TV ≤ tau** across a grid of tau values and many input pairs; **log Z_p inside [0, eta_tau]** on every draw; and tau = 0 reproducing R_D exactly (§4.16.5). A failure there is an implementation bug, not a verdict on the approach.
>
> The first gate that could **kill the approach** is gate 3: transition constants must give a measured switch mass of order **0.1 to 1** at realistic separations, and a sampled block must be demonstrably **not** a single donor copied verbatim — the gate PanMixer's shipped sampler fails **300 times out of 300** (measured in this project). If, with correct constants and a real genetic map, chr21 still comes back as single-donor copies over long stretches, then the mosaic that the whole utility argument rests on does not exist at this scale, and the method as designed does not do what it claims. Two notes on reading it. The **GRCh38 genetic map is now in hand** (`external/genetic_maps/chr21.b38.gmap`, acquired 2026-09-27), so the gate can be run against the published transition model. But the gate's stated threshold is **mis-specified** and must be restated first: "order 0.1 to 1" is wrong for chain positions about 12 bp apart, where the biologically correct value is the measured 0.0026 (§4.16.5).
>
> The first **utility** number after that is `target_fidelity` at a stated tau, measured against the two mandatory columns — the floor at tau = 0 and the ceiling of the target's own path. A tau whose fidelity is indistinguishable from the floor is a tau that published nothing.

The last extends the concentration table far enough to see where the tilt stops being visible.

> **Question** — *"Add a row to the table for m = 1,000 and m = 10,000, so I can see where the tilt becomes visible."*
>
> Extending the table at tau = 0.5, where eta_tau = 0.549306 and the per-block factor is exp(eta_tau/m) (all derived):
>
> | m — blocks carrying the weight | per-block bonus factor | reading |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1 | **1.7321** | the full budget on one block; the best candidate's odds improve by 1.73× |
> | 10 | **1.0565** | a visible nudge |
> | 100 | **1.0055** | marginal |
> | 1,000 | **1.0005** | not visible |
> | 10,000 | **1.00005** | indistinguishable from 1 |
> | 100,757 (all of chr21) | **1.0000055** | indistinguishable from 1 |
>
> The crossover is between m = 10 and m = 100: anything beyond a few hundred blocks and the tilt has stopped steering the draw. That is the arithmetic behind the claim that budget allocation is the open research question rather than a tuning detail — and it is also why "protect the whole chromosome uniformly" and "produce an output that resembles the target" are, at these numbers, close to mutually exclusive.

| A3. Map the target | External genome g, graph G | p = Map(g, G) | The private input path p, split into blocks p_1..p_T | The only private object in the system |
| A4. Compute potentials | p, the traversals h_{t,j}, weights beta_t, similarity phi_t | psi_t(j) = exp(eta_tau·beta_t·phi_t(p_t, h_{t,j})) for every block t and state j | A T-by-K table of numbers in [1, exp(eta_tau·beta_t)] | This is the *only* place the private target touches the model, and its influence is bounded by construction |
| A5. Forward filtering | rho, A_t, psi_t | Left-to-right recursion with per-block renormalization; the stay/switch collapse makes each block O(K) | All T forward vectors alpha_1..alpha_T, plus log Z_p = sum of log c_t | Computes the exact conditionals needed for exact sampling; storing *all* alphas is what makes a backward pass possible |
| A6. Backward sampling | alpha_1..alpha_T, transitions | Draw Z_T from alpha_T; then for t = T−1 down to 1 draw Z_t from weights alpha_t(i)·A_{t+1}(i, Z_{t+1}) | One exact draw Z_1..Z_T from Q_p | Sampling (not argmax) is what keeps the output distribution non-degenerate and therefore bounded in TV |
| A7. Emit the path | Z_1..Z_T, traversals h_{t,j} | Concatenate: p̃ = h_{1,Z_1} ‖ … ‖ h_{T,Z_T} | One sanitized path | Deterministic post-processing cannot increase TV, so the guarantee carries through |
| A8. Cache and serve | p̃ | Store the single released path; serve it for all future queries about this genome | A frozen release | Condition 4; independent re-releases would multiply the attacker's evidence, with only a loose k·tau bound to fall back on |


> **Question** — *"A8 says cache the released path and serve it forever. Where does the cache live, who can read it, and what happens when the graph is updated and every path needs remapping?"*
>
> The cache is an operational requirement of condition 4 rather than a component of the mechanism, and it has to be somewhere that guarantees the **same** release is served on every subsequent request for that genome — one release per genome means one artefact, not one policy. Who may read it is the same question as who may read the release, since it **is** the release.
>
> A graph update is the hard case and the answer is uncomfortable: remapping the target against a new graph produces a **new** path p′, and releasing a draw for p′ is a **second release of the same genome**. It is therefore a condition-4 event, bounded only by the loose k·tau composition, and it invalidates the cache in the sense that the cache can no longer be the whole story. There is no mechanism-level fix on offer here; composition across releases is recorded as an open question.

> **Question** — *"A3 'Map the target' comes after A1 and A2. Is that ordering load-bearing, or just how the table reads?"*
>
> Load-bearing. A1 and A2 build Y_D and R_D, and conditions 1 and 2 require both to be fixed **before the target is seen** — an output support or a baseline chosen after looking at the target is the disjoint-support failure of §4.4, not a mild bias. So the order is a requirement, not a convenience.
>
> There is a practical test of whether an implementation respects it: if tau = 0 does not reproduce R_D exactly, something downstream of A3 leaked into A1 or A2. That is one of the four checks in exit gate 1 (§4.16.5), and it is the check that would catch our current frequency-table problem (§4.4), where an artefact built from a panel containing the target means A2 was not target-free after all.

### In plain words — Section 4, Part A

We want a privacy guarantee that does not depend on guessing which attacker will show up, so instead of testing attacks we reason about probability distributions. The mechanism never edits the target's genome path; it draws an entirely new path from a distribution over paths that the public cohort already supports. That distribution has two parts: a public baseline model, R_D, built only from the public graph and cohort, and a bounded utility score u(p, y) in [0, 1] saying how useful a candidate output y would be for the target's real path p. We multiply the baseline probability of each candidate by exp(eta_tau · u), where eta_tau = arctanh(tau) and tau is a privacy dial we choose in advance, then renormalize. Because u can only range over [0, 1], the bonus factor can only range over a factor of exp(eta_tau), which caps how much any private input can bend the public baseline. That cap turns into a total variation distance of at most tau between the output distributions of *any* two possible inputs, which in turn caps any attacker's distinguishing accuracy at (1 + tau)/2 — so tau = 0.1 means at most 55% accuracy on a task where guessing gets 50%. Implementing it is standard machinery: the baseline is a Li–Stephens-style copying HMM over T chain positions and K cohort haplotype states, the tilt becomes a per-position bonus factor, and we sample exactly with forward-filtering/backward-sampling in O(T·K) time — about 30 million operations on our rebuilt chr21, where T = 340,824 variants (the decided chain unit) and K = 88 haplotypes, the full cohort, because the target is external and nothing is held out. The proposal lists three conditions the proof depends on — the output menu must be fixed without looking at the target, the baseline must be learned from public data only, and the utility must be bounded — and we add a fourth operational one, that only one release per genome may be made and that we must *sample* rather than take the most likely path, because a deterministic mechanism has total variation distance 1 and therefore no guarantee at all. The honest catch is that a whole-chromosome worst-case budget spread over hundreds of thousands of positions leaves almost no tilt at any one of them — and note that the *total* tilt is invariant in T, so changing the chain unit redistributes the budget rather than shrinking it — so how to allocate that budget is an open research question rather than a solved one, and no value of tau has yet been run end to end here. One last division to carry out of Part A, because it is the thing most easily blurred: Theorem 1 and its corollaries are **proved on paper**; the v1 sampler that would implement them is **specified but not built** (Part C); and every utility number, attack number and fidelity number for our arm is **neither** — unmeasured, at every value of tau.


> **Question** — *"The summary ends on 'no value of tau has yet been run end to end here.' Good. Can the summary also say which parts are proved, which are implemented, and which are neither?"*
>
> It should, and the sentence now added at the close of that paragraph does it in outline. Here is the same split at full length.
>
> **Proved on paper.** Theorem 1 and its corollaries: the tilt's bonus factor is bounded, Z_p lies in [1, exp(eta_tau)], R_D cancels in the likelihood ratio, the pointwise ratio is at most exp(2·eta_tau), and therefore TV ≤ tanh(eta_tau) = tau, giving an optimal equal-prior attacker at most (1 + tau)/2. (Two typesetting slips in our own proposal PDF lose a "≤ e" before a superscript in Theorem 1 and in the proof's first line; the mathematics is unaffected, and the reason they are recorded at all is that a one-sided bound transcribed literally into an assertion would pass on every input.)
>
> **Specified but not implemented.** The v1 sampler, which Part C sets out in full: one chain over the whole chromosome with the individual variant as the unit (model C), so no anchors, no blocks, no allele-frequency branch and no singleton special case; exact tilted FFBS storing every alpha column in float64; transition constants re-derived from the published Methods; and no knapsack. Pinned 2026-09-25, **narrowed 2026-09-28**, status **In Progress**.
>
> **Neither — unmeasured.** Every utility number, every attack number, every fidelity number for our arm. **No tau has been run and no RanPanMixer number exists.** The GRCh38 genetic map is now in hand (2026-09-27), so a real-data switch mass under corrected constants is finally computable — but it has not been computed, and exit gate 3's stated threshold needs restating before it means anything (§4.16.5).

## Part B — Our mechanism compared with PanMixer

### 4.11 The conceptual comparison

| Dimension | PanMixer (Section 3) | Our mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Who is protected** | An individual who **is a contributor** to the released pangenome graph | An **external** individual, not in the cohort and not in the graph |
| **What is private** | That contributor's haplotypes, which are already inside the public graph | The target's mapped path p through an unchanged public graph |
| **What is released** | The **cohort VCF with the contributor's genotype column rewritten** — i.e. a modified reference resource | A **standalone sanitized path**; nothing is ever written back into the reference |
| **Release rule** | Per block, generate one candidate; run a knapsack-style linear-programming optimization to decide replace-or-keep; apply the selected replacements | Resample the **entire path** from a tilted distribution; no per-block keep/replace decision exists |
| **Blocks not selected** | Released **verbatim** | There is no such category — every block is resampled |
| **Privacy quantity** | Per-block Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) as named in the code, which for an obfuscated block reduces to the self-information −log p(block); "high score = high risk" | tau, a worst-case bound on the total variation distance between output distributions |
| **Type of guarantee** | Average-case, empirical: a measured per-block privacy score plus demonstrated failure of specific attacks (gap-score linkage; membership inference in the published version) | Worst-case, analytic: holds for all admissible input pairs and all attackers, given the conditions of §4.8 |
| **The control knob** | A utility-loss **capacity** (the fraction of a utility-loss denominator the user is willing to spend) | tau, chosen in advance, with the reading "attacker accuracy ≤ (1 + tau)/2" |
| **Role of randomness** | Randomness is present (candidate sampling) but **no formal claim is proved from it**. Note this is *not* the same as saying the seed does not matter: seed secrecy remains operationally necessary for PanMixer, because an attacker who knew the seed could replay the algorithm on candidate inputs until the output matched. Section 2 makes that operational argument; this row is only about what the *formal* claims rest on | Load-bearing in both senses: the randomness *is* the guarantee, and the draw must stay secret |
| **Where the private input enters** | Through the per-block privacy score (the scorer reads the target's own haplotype) and hence the knapsack objective and selection, and through the stacker, which copies the target's unselected blocks. Note that in the **released code** the candidate generator itself is target-independent given the panel: `sample_block_prior` is an unconditional prior draw over the without-target panel, with no comparison to the original and no rejection loop. The published paper describes a constraint the released code does not implement (measured in this project by reading the pinned checkout; see `docs/memory.md`, 2026-09-18) | Only the bounded local potentials psi_t |
| **Operating point** | Chosen empirically: the published paper reports eps_private = 0.002, defined there as the **largest** privacy-risk value at which all targets could no longer be linked (the preprint's wording was the reverse; use the published definition) | Chosen a priori as a policy parameter |

**The two privacy quantities are formally incomparable.** PanMixer's per-block score is an *average-case information quantity* attached to a pair of haplotypes; our tau is a *worst-case bound on a whole output distribution*. Neither implies the other, and there is no conversion formula. A head-to-head comparison between the two systems can only be made on **measured empirical attack success** — running the same corrected attack against both outputs and comparing — never by comparing a privacy score to tau directly.


> **Question** — *"'The two privacy quantities are formally incomparable' and 'a head-to-head can only be made on measured empirical attack success'. So what actually goes in the comparison table in our paper? Two columns with an empty cell where the other's metric would be?"*
>
> Metrics as rows, the two systems as columns — and yes, some cells are explicitly marked not-comparable rather than left blank, because a blank reads as missing data.
>
> | row | PanMixer | ours |
> |---|---|---|
> | privacy parameter | `eps_private`, an empirical operating point on the paper's privacy-risk axis (whose construction this project has not reconstructed) | tau, a worst-case TV bound; **no conversion exists** |
> | measured attack success (gap score, corrected) | to be measured per operating point | to be measured per operating point |
> | `target_fidelity` | to be measured — **not yet run in either arm** | to be measured — **not yet run in either arm** |
> | `af_loss` (cohort allele-frequency distortion) | published **0.006 / 0.006 / 0.005** at eps_private = 0.002; not reproduced here | **not defined for our release object** — we never edit the cohort, so there is no cohort distortion to measure. Not a metric this project computes |
> | `ld_loss` (cohort LD distortion) | published **0.003** at eps_private = 0.002; not reproduced here | **not defined for our release object**, same reason |
> | read-mapping fidelity | published **77.82 / 95.61 / 77.01** at eps_private = 0.002; obfuscated arm not run here | to be measured at a stated tau |
> | floor column | `to_empty` / full-resample baseline | tau = 0 |
> | ceiling column | the target's own path | the target's own path |
>
> Note the two "not defined" cells, which are the point of writing the table out rather than assuming metric parity. They are **not** "identically zero" and they are **not** missing data: `af_loss` and `ld_loss` ask how far overwriting one cohort member's genotype column moved the cohort's own statistics, and our deliverable is a standalone path that leaves the cohort file untouched, so the question has no referent (`docs/terms.md`). The reverse asymmetry is just as real and is ours to build: **two-target indistinguishability** — run the mechanism on targets X and Y, estimate the empirical TV between the two release distributions, check it against tau — is the test our guarantee actually makes, and **nothing in PanMixer's suite tests it**.
>
> The rules that make the table honest are already decided: align the arms **only** on measured empirical attack success; report **both** frontiers; run attacks **per operating point, never pooled**; use **R ≥ 10 seeds per (subject, level) in both arms**; and stamp every result with graph-plus-cohort identity, tau, and utility identity — plus, for comparison-arm runs only, which individual stood in as target (project decisions, `docs/plan.md`). Two numbers are comparable only if their stamps match.

> **Question** — *"The 'control knob' row says capacity is 'the fraction of a utility-loss denominator'. That denominator is the unreachable one from Step 15. So the knob doesn't mean what it appears to mean — should that row say so?"*
>
> Yes, carry the Step 15 caveat into the row. Capacity is a fraction of a **constructed** denominator — twice the sum of 1/support over every site, **9,288.85** for HG00438 on our chr21 rebuild — and that total is not reachable, because it presumes every site on both strands is changed. Capacity 0.1 spent **928.92**, exactly **10.000%** of it; capacity 0.5 spent only **33.1%** of it, because the knapsack **saturated** (all measured in this project).
>
> So the knob is monotone but not proportional, and it has a ceiling well below 1 that the knob itself does not reveal. Our tau has the opposite character: it is a bound with an exact meaning at every value and no saturation, and it does not tell you how much of the genome changed. The two knobs are not two settings of one dial, and the row should say which kind of object each one is.

> **Question** — *"The 'role of randomness' row says their candidate generator is target-independent given the panel. If their candidate draw is target-independent and ours is a tilted draw, then ours depends on the target more than theirs does. Is our mechanism less private at the sampling step?"*
>
> The premise is right and the conclusion does not follow, and this is worth spelling out because it is the sharpest possible objection to our design.
>
> Their candidate really is target-independent: `sample_block_prior` is an **unconditional prior draw** with no comparison to the original and no rejection loop, despite the paper describing a constraint to differ (measured in this project). So at the candidate-generation step they leak nothing. Their leak is entirely in the two steps after it — the **knapsack selection**, which ranks blocks by the rarity of the target's own alleles, and the **verbatim republication** of everything unselected, which makes two inputs differing in an unselected block perfectly distinguishable.
>
> Ours is the mirror image. The tilt is the **only** channel through which the target enters, and it is bounded pointwise by construction: every candidate's weight is multiplied by a factor in [1, exp(eta_tau·beta_t)], nothing is selected on the basis of the target, and nothing is republished verbatim. So the comparison is not "which step touches the target" but "is the dependence bounded". A small unbounded dependence in the selection is worse than a large bounded one in the sampler — which is the whole content of §4.12.

### 4.12 Why PanMixer's design cannot satisfy our guarantee

This is not a criticism of PanMixer; it is solving a different problem and never claimed a pairwise TV bound. But the reason is instructive, and it is exactly the reason our release rule looks the way it does.

Recall Section 3's **stacker** step: it copies the target's original haplotypes, then overwrites only the blocks the optimizer selected. Every unselected block is republished **byte-for-byte as it was in the target**. We verified this directly in the pinned checkout: `stack()` starts from `original_haplotypes.copy()` and writes into it only at positions where the solution vector equals 1.

Now run the argument. The output alphabet is paths over all T blocks. Consider a block b that the optimizer does not select — and there are many. The optimizer's decision vector has one variable per (block, haplotype) pair, so on our chr21 it has 100,757 × 2 = **201,514** decisions. At capacity 0.1 the shipped run selects **47,070** of them, i.e. about 23%, leaving roughly **77%** of the target's block-haplotypes republished verbatim; the stacker's own accounting on the same run reports **68,335 of 650,541 allele slots changed (10.50%)**, so about 89.5% of individual allele slots come through untouched. (Measured in this project, one subject HG00438 on chr21, rebuilt on the 30x GRCh38 panel; raw figures in `logs/pm_obf.log`, summarized in `docs/memory.md` under 2026-09-18.)

> **Defect** — the verbatim share is larger than the move count suggests, which strengthens the argument rather than weakening it. Of the **47,070** moves the LP selected at capacity 0.1, **14,526 — 30.9% —** changed **nothing at all**: the sampled replacement was allele-for-allele identical to what the target already carried, because the degenerate transitions copy one donor verbatim and on a block of common alleles that donor often carries the target's own alleles. So the number of block-haplotypes that come through unchanged is not ~77% but closer to ~84% (derived: 201,514 − 47,070 + 14,526, over 201,514). Two things must be kept separate here, because an earlier draft of this project's notes conflated them. The **move** share is 30.9%; the share of the reported privacy credit those no-op moves carry is **7.0%**, not 30.9%, because they concentrate on low-scoring blocks — median `eps_j` **0.3898** for the moves that changed nothing against **2.1904** for the moves that changed something, over a sample of 3,000 of the 47,070. And the utility axis is **honest**: `eta_j` is computed as a sum over *altered* variants, so a no-op move is correctly charged **0.00000**. What is inflated is the privacy numerator, and therefore the privacy-per-utility ratio — not the utility. (All measured in this project, capacity 0.1, subject HG00438, chr21; `docs/memory.md` 2026-09-24, `docs/bugs.md`.) For us the consequence is a reporting rule rather than a defect to fix: our own arm must report **measured allele change**, never a summed privacy score, as its privacy numerator.

**Two questions this immediately raises.**

> **Question** — *"Hold on. I now have three percentages pointing three different ways: about 77% of block-haplotype decisions never selected, about 89.5% of individual allele slots untouched, and now about 84% of block-haplotypes unchanged in fact. Which one is the honest headline?"* None of the three, for the purpose §4.12 is arguing — but take them apart first, with their denominators, because they are three different counts and not three views of one. **47,070 of 201,514** (block, haplotype) decisions were selected at capacity 0.1, so about 23% selected and about 77% not (measured in this project). **68,335 of 650,541** allele slots changed, i.e. **10.50%** changed and about 89.5% untouched (measured in this project). And **201,514 − 47,070 + 14,526 = 168,970 of 201,514**, about **84%**, is the share of block-haplotypes that end up allele-for-allele identical to the target's own once the no-op moves are counted as unchanged (derived). The reason the slot share is so much lower than the decision share is a mechanism worth holding on to: the optimizer buys privacy value per unit of utility cost, and a block holding few variants costs little, so the selected blocks are the **small** ones — a large share of decisions can move a small share of slots. The same fact stated as a rate: 68,335 changed slots over 47,070 selected moves is **1.45** slots per selected move, and over the **32,544** moves that changed anything it is **2.10** slots each (both derived), which is what you would expect when **88.4% of dictionary entries** are singletons with exactly one slot available to change (measured in this project). Now the headline. §4.12's argument needs none of these numbers. It needs **one** block that is unselected under both of two admissible inputs, and it needs that block to exist with probability 1. The percentages say how large the effect is in practice; the disjoint-support argument would go through at 1%.

> **Question** — *"Why does the optimizer ever pick a move that changes nothing? Isn't the replacement supposed to differ from what the target already carries?"* The **published Methods say it is**, and the released code does not implement that. `docs/memory.md` carries the correction in as many words: the paper states the candidate is constrained to differ from the target's own block, while `sample_block_prior` in the released code is an unconditional prior draw with no comparison to the original and no rejection loop (measured in this project by reading the pinned checkout, 2026-09-18). Combine that with the degenerate transitions of departure (b), which copy one donor haplotype verbatim across the whole block, and on a block of common alleles that donor frequently carries exactly what the target carries — hence **14,526 of 47,070 (30.9%)** moves that change nothing (measured in this project). Note the framing, which is a standing rule here rather than politeness: this is the released code differing from the published Methods, not a claim that any published figure is wrong, and we have reproduced none of their figures. For us the failure mode is unreachable, because v1 has no moves to select at all (§4.16.2, replacement 5); what survives is the reporting rule at the end of the callout above.

Take two admissible inputs p and q that are identical everywhere except at block b, where they carry different alleles, and suppose b is unselected under both — easy to arrange: pick a block of common alleles with negligible privacy score, which the optimizer will never prioritize.

Then:

- Every output in the support of Q_p carries p's alleles at block b, with probability 1.
- Every output in the support of Q_q carries q's alleles at block b, with probability 1.
- The two supports are **disjoint**.
- Therefore TV(Q_p, Q_q) = **1**, its maximum possible value.
- Therefore the attacker bound (1 + TV)/2 = **1**: an attacker who simply reads block b identifies the input with certainty.

No tuning of the capacity fixes this. Raising the capacity selects more blocks but never all of them, and in fact the knapsack **saturates below its budget**: at capacity 0.5 the same run makes 177,865 moves for 33.1% utility loss rather than the requested 50%, because every move with positive value already fits (both measured in this project; `logs/pm_obf.log`, `docs/memory.md` 2026-09-18 for the 33.1% and 2026-09-26 for the 177,865). And even at full capacity the *conditional* structure remains: which blocks get replaced is itself a function of the target, so the selection pattern leaks.

The general lesson, and the design rule we derive from it: **any mechanism that passes part of its private input through unchanged has total variation distance 1 between some pair of inputs.** A bounded-TV mechanism must resample everything. That is why our release rule cannot be "edit some blocks" and must be "draw a whole path."

**The symmetric honesty.** Our mechanism cannot produce PanMixer's deliverable either. PanMixer's output is a *reference resource* — a pangenome VCF or graph that third parties will genotype against — and it has to keep 44 individuals' columns mutually consistent inside one file. Our output is a single sanitized path for one external individual. Neither tool subsumes the other; they address related but distinct scenarios, exactly as the proposal's Discussion states.


> **Question** — *"'Even at full capacity the selection pattern is itself a function of the target, so the selection leaks.' Can you make that concrete? What does an attacker learn from knowing which blocks were selected?"*
>
> They learn a **rarity fingerprint of the target**. The LP maximises sum_j `eps_j`·x_j subject to one budget constraint, so it buys in descending order of `eps_j`/`eta_j` — and `eps_j` is computed from **the target's own original block**. A block is selected because the target's alleles there were surprising under the cohort model. So the selection vector is, read directly, a list of "the places where this person is unusual", which is close to a description of the very thing the release was supposed to hide.
>
> How an attacker reads it: any diff that reveals which rows changed reveals the selection (Step 19's question about two capacities is one route; comparison against the cohort VCF is another, since unselected rows are byte-identical to the original file's other releases). The block-1077 trace makes the flavour concrete in the other direction too — a block **82 of 88** donors share scored `eps_j` = **0.0711**, near the bottom of the range, because it is exactly what everybody else has (measured in this project).
>
> This is worth stating as the general design rule rather than as a PanMixer finding: **a mechanism whose output distribution depends on the private input through a selection decision cannot have a bounded worst-case guarantee, no matter how good the candidate generator is.** Bounding the candidate is not enough; the choice of whether to use it has to be bounded too.

> **Question** — *"The 'symmetric honesty' paragraph — is our mechanism actually less useful for the thing pangenomes exist for? Their output goes into a reference resource; ours doesn't go anywhere yet. Who is the consumer of a single sanitized path?"*
>
> Less useful for **that** thing, yes, and it is better to name the consumer than to argue the point.
>
> What PanMixer delivers is a cohort VCF with one column rewritten — an artefact that slots back into the published resource, so the resource keeps its shape and a contributor's column stops being a fingerprint. We cannot deliver that: our output is one path, and writing it back into the reference is not what our guarantee is about.
>
> What we deliver is for an **external individual**: a cohort-supported path that can be queried against the public graph on their behalf. Concretely, that means genotyping or mapping-based analysis for one person who wants to use a pangenome resource without contributing their real path to it — the person hands over a release instead of a genome, and the release carries a stated bound on what it discloses. The two deliverables serve related but distinct scenarios, which is the proposal's own framing.
>
> Two honest riders. Our output **cannot** carry the target's private novel sequence at all (§4.3), so it is unsuitable for any question about that. And whether a single sanitized path is useful enough to be worth publishing is **unmeasured** — no tau has been run, so there is no fidelity number to put against the claim.

### 4.13 The reuse map

Keyed to the pipeline stages walked through in Section 3. Verdicts: **REUSE AS IS** / **ADAPT** / **REPLACE** / **EVALUATION ONLY**.

Two things to keep straight throughout this table, because several numbers depend on them:

- **Two different 1000 Genomes releases are in play.** The shipped pipeline downloads 1000 Genomes **Phase 3** (2,504 samples, build **GRCh37**, release 20130502). This project substituted the **30x GRCh38** panel (3,202 samples; 1,002,753 chr21 records in the copy registered in `docs/data.md`). Every panel-derived number below says which panel produced it.
- **Block counts are entries, not variants.** On our rebuilt chr21 the block dictionary has **100,757 entries** covering **340,824 variants**. Of those entries, **89,087 (88.4% of entries)** are singletons holding exactly one variant each. Those singletons account for 89,087 of 340,824 variants = **26.1% of variants**. Separately, **10.4% of entries** carry two or more anchor variants and therefore take the HMM code path, and those entries hold **71.4% of variants**. The 100,757 entries are an expansion of **14,137 LD blocks produced by PLINK's `--blocks` routine**. Never say "88.4% of variants": always name the denominator. (All measured in this project; `docs/memory.md` 2026-09-18, and re-verified by counting `blocks_dict.json` directly.)

- **The block structure is a comparison-arm asset, not an input to our mechanism.** Under the decided chain architecture (model C, §4.16) the chain unit is the individual variant, so **there are no LD blocks in the mechanism, no PLINK run, and no external panel in the mechanism's input set at all**. Blocks are retained for two things only: the model-B fallback, and parity runs against PanMixer. Everything in this subsection about them should be read in that scope. Where blocks *are* used, "inherit" means inherit the method, not the artifact — a statement about the *procedure* — run PLINK's `--blocks` on a 1000 Genomes panel, then assign each pangenome record to a block by its start position — and **not** about the particular `blocks_dict.json` the shipped pipeline produces. ⚠ And they must not be computed from the 44-sample cohort: measured 2026-09-28, that gives 7,858 blocks covering 50.4% of variants against 14,137 / 74.2% from the panel. If blocks are wanted at all, they need a large external panel. The shipped artifact is computed on the **GRCh37 Phase 3** panel and then used to bin **GRCh38** positions, which is departure (a); our artifact is recomputed with the same procedure on the **30x GRCh38** panel. The boundaries, and therefore T, are a function of whichever panel PLINK ran on, so the distinction is load-bearing rather than pedantic. (This project has measured the block structure only on the 30x GRCh38 rebuild — **14,137** PLINK blocks, **100,757** dictionary entries, measured in this project; the Phase 3 block structure has not been measured here, so no comparison of the two T values can be quoted.) Everywhere below, "inherited unchanged" means *same method, artifact rebuilt on the build-matched panel*. (Measured in this project; `docs/memory.md` 2026-09-18, `docs/data.md`, section 3.)

> **Question** — *"So 'inherit unchanged' means we rebuild the artifact. Then which T am I actually inheriting? Every block number in this document is 100,757, which is the 30x GRCh38 rebuild. Do we even know what theirs is?"* We do not. **Working the utility weights on a real block.** The (beta_t, phi_t) construction in the first row of that table is short enough to look like notation rather than arithmetic, so here it is performed on the one block Section 3 already traces end to end.

Block 1077 on our chr21 rebuild holds **5 variants** at rows 34816–34820, POS 13,000,333–13,000,621, and `support(v)` is **87 of 88** for every one of them, so each variant's weight 1/support(v) is **0.01149** (all measured in this project; `docs/memory.md` 2026-09-24). Then:

- **W_1077** = 5 × (1/87) = **0.05747** (derived; the per-variant weight quoted above, 0.01149, is 1/87 rounded to four decimal places, so multiply the unrounded value). This is the block's total utility weight, and it is also exactly PanMixer's `eta_j` for the hypothetical move that changed all five variants.
- **phi_1077** if the sampled block reproduces the target's allele at **3 of the 5** variants = (3/87) / (5/87) = **0.600** (derived). If it reproduces all five, phi = 1; if none, phi = 0. Because every variant here carries the same weight, phi is just the matched fraction; in a block with unequal supports it is the *weighted* matched fraction, and the rare variants — the ones with small support — carry the most weight.
- **beta_1077** = W_1077 / (sum of W over all 100,757 blocks). That denominator is not stated as such anywhere in `docs/`, but it is recoverable from a number that is: Step 15's budget denominator for HG00438 on our chr21 rebuild is **9,288.85** (measured in this project), and it is defined as *twice* the sum of `utility_loss` over the chromosome's sites, so the single-strand sum — which is exactly the sum of W over all blocks — is **4,644.43** (derived). That gives **beta_1077 = 0.05747 / 4,644.43 = 1.24e-5** (derived), against the uniform share 1/100,757 = **9.92e-6** (derived): about **1.25×** the uniform weight. Treat that as an illustration of the arithmetic and not as a committed scheme — which scheme we use is undecided (§4.16.8, item 5).

> **Open question** — beta_t = W_t / sum(W) hands the largest share of the utility budget to blocks packed with **rare** variants, because 1/support(v) is largest exactly where support is smallest. Rare variants are also the most identifying ones. So a weight-proportional beta makes the release try hardest to reproduce precisely the material that most identifies the target. Two things must be said about that, and they point in opposite directions. First, it does **not** breach the guarantee: tau bounds distinguishability whatever beta does, because the bound only ever used the facts that u lies in [0, 1] and that beta is fixed without looking at the target (§4.7). Second, it does decide *where the output is most faithful*, and "most faithful exactly where it is most identifying" is a design property worth choosing deliberately rather than inheriting from PanMixer's cost model. Whether weight-proportional, uniform, or length-weighted beta is right for us is undecided; see §4.16.8.

One thing the table above does not say at all, and it is the largest gap in it.

> **Caution** — none of the rows in that table say what happens when a donor carries no called allele at one of the block's variants. That is not an oversight in the table; it is an open modelling decision, and it is large enough to have its own subsection (§4.17). It also has a **computational** consequence that belongs here: the RENORMALISE option drops a donor from the state distribution at the positions where it is missing, which makes the effective state set position-dependent. The stay/switch collapse of §4.10.2 survives that — it only needs one stay probability, one switch probability and a running total over the states that are live at that position — but the running total must then be recomputed per position rather than reused, and the transition normalisation must use the live count rather than a constant n. That is a real interaction between §4.17 and §4.10.2 and it should be written into the sampler deliberately, not discovered in a debugger.

**Two more questions about that arithmetic.**

> **Question** — *"Block 1077 has every variant at the same support, so phi is just the matched fraction and the weighting does nothing. Work one where the supports differ, so I can see the weighting actually bite — and show me beta_t and the tilt it buys, not just phi."* Fair, and the flat case is genuinely misleading about what phi_t does. Here is the arithmetic on a **hypothetical** block of three variants at supports 88, 40 and 4 — the three supports are chosen to make the weighting visible and are **not measured from any real block**, though the range is realistic (every variant in block 1077 sits at support 87 of 88, measured in this project). The per-variant weights are 1/88 = **0.011364**, 1/40 = **0.025000** and 1/4 = **0.250000**, so **W_t = 0.286364** (all derived). Notice how lopsided that already is: the support-4 variant supplies **87.3%** of the block's weight (derived). Now three outputs, and phi_t is the fraction of W_t the output reproduces:
>
> | output reproduces the target's allele at | phi_t | reading |
> |---|---|---|
> | the two **common** variants (supports 88 and 40) | (0.011364 + 0.025000)/0.286364 = **0.127** | two of three matched, and it scores badly |
> | the **rare** variant only (support 4) | 0.250000/0.286364 = **0.873** | one of three matched, and it scores well |
> | the rare one **and** the support-40 one | (0.250000 + 0.025000)/0.286364 = **0.960** | two of three, chosen differently |
>
> So matching one variant of three scores about **6.9×** what matching two of three scores, when the one is the rare one (derived). That is the whole content of "weighted matched fraction", and it is also the exact property the **Open question** above is uneasy about: the weight lands on the rare material, which is the identifying material. And for the budget this buys: beta_t = 0.286364 / 4,644.43 = **6.17e-5** (derived), about **6.2×** the uniform share of 9.92e-6, so the per-block tilt at tau = 0.5 is exp(0.549306 × 6.17e-5) = **1.0000339** (derived) — larger than the uniform block's 1.0000055 and still indistinguishable from 1. The §4.10.4 tension does not go away when the weights are non-uniform; it concentrates.

> **Question** — *"What if support(v) = 0 at a position — then w_v = 1/support(v) divides by zero, and under model B a whole block could have W_t = 0."* It has now been checked, and the answer is that the case is real but vanishingly rare: **exactly 1 of 340,824 chr21 sites** has all 88 donors missing, and **89 sites** leave one donor or fewer (measured in this project, 2026-09-28). So the guard is an assertion plus one documented behaviour, not a research programme — a position with no called donor offers the output nothing to preserve or lose, and the natural treatment is to give it beta_v = 0 and leave it out of the normalisation. Do **not** import the nearby measurement as reassurance for a different quantity: `docs/` records that **0 of 319,092** checked sites have allele frequency f_v = 0 (measured in this project), but allele frequency is estimated from an external panel while support is a count over cohort donors, so that number says nothing at all about support. Note also that support itself needs no recomputation — the VCF's own `INFO/AN` is bit-identical to the count over the matrix across all 340,824 records (measured in this project, 2026-09-28).

A note on the single-file standalone, since it is the most convenient entry point into their code. `tools/panmixer/obfuscate.py` is **not** a partial reimplementation of a numbered subset of pipeline steps: its own docstring says it "Runs the full pipeline: PMI/utility computation -> optimize -> stack -> VCF output" for one subject on one chromosome, and it **requires pre-existing inputs** including `blocks_dict.json` and `allele_frequencies.npy`. So it consumes the preprocessing artifacts rather than producing them. It is the file we executed to obtain the measurements cited above.


> **Question** — *"beta_t = W_t / sum W_t means a block packed with rare variants gets most of the utility budget. But rare variants are the identifying ones. Are we spending our whole tilt budget on exactly the material we're trying not to disclose?"*
>
> This is the sharpest question in Part B and it deserves a real answer rather than a reassurance, so here it is in three parts.
>
> First, the premise is correct. W_t is the sum of 1/support(v) over a block's variants, so a low-support — rare, or poorly called — variant contributes a large term, and beta_t = W_t / sum_b W_b therefore concentrates weight on blocks containing such variants.
>
> Second, beta_t allocates **utility importance, not disclosure**. tau bounds the distinguishability of the whole released path irrespective of where beta sits, because the bound follows from u lying in [0, 1] and from nothing about how u is composed. So the answer to "are we disclosing more there" is **no**, in the formal sense that matters for the theorem.
>
> Third, the part not to wave away: the allocation **does** decide which material the output tries hardest to reproduce. A rare-variant-weighted beta makes the release most faithful exactly where the target is most identifying. That is not a violation of the bound, and it is a legitimate thing to be uneasy about — a release that is bounded but optimised for fidelity on identifying material is a different product from one optimised for fidelity on common material. **Whether that is the right objective is an open question**, and the beta_t scheme is undecided (§4.16.8, item 5). Note also the inherited blind spot: 1/support(v) is per **record**, so a 257 kb allele and a 1 bp SNP contribute the same weight — reusing PanMixer's weight inherits its scale blindness (**3.13 Mb** rewritten on a run that reported 10.5% of allele slots changed, measured in this project).

> **Question** — *"The row doesn't mention missing data at all. If a donor carries −1 at one of the three variants, what is phi_t? RENORMALISE changes the state distribution, which changes A_t — does that break the stay/switch collapse and the O(T·K) cost?"*
>
> Two answers, and the second is a real interaction nothing in `docs/` has yet written down.
>
> What phi does there: **the policy is RENORMALISE** (decided 2026-09-28) — the missing donor is dropped from the state distribution at that position and the remaining donors are renormalised, so it is simply not a candidate there. The three rejected alternatives are **WILDCARD** (treat missing as matching whatever the target has), **MISMATCH** (treat it as a difference), and **RESTRICT** (drop the position from the chain, which is PanMixer's anchor rule in effect and is retained as a **comparison baseline** because it is the published behaviour). Each has a bias, and RENORMALISE's is stated rather than hidden: it is right for a not-applicable call and wrong for an assembly gap, where the donor almost certainly has an allele we merely failed to record. WILDCARD would make poorly-assembled donors universally attractive; MISMATCH would invent differences the data never asserted (§4.17).
>
> The computational interaction: RENORMALISE makes the **live state set position-dependent**, which is a departure from a transition matrix with one value on the diagonal and one value everywhere else. The collapse itself survives, because it only ever needed one stay probability, one switch probability and a running total over the states that are live at that position — so the O(T·K) cost of §4.10.2 is preserved — but the running total has to be recomputed at each such position rather than carried forward, and the transition normalisation must use the **live** count rather than a constant n. ⚠ Note that the transition matrix `A` is **never materialised** in any case — Li–Stephens `A = (1−s_t)·I + (s_t/K)·J` is rank-one plus diagonal, so each forward step is O(K) from one scalar sum. What would genuinely cost O(K²) per position, about **780 million** operations per haplotype against **8.87 million** at the block-unit count (derived), is a matrix with K distinct rows — donor-specific transition rates, which is a different feature and is discussed at the end of §4.10.2. The point to carry is that the missingness policy is simultaneously a modelling decision, a privacy decision (§4.8, condition 1) and a code-structure decision.
>
> Scale of the problem the policy addresses: the mean missingness at the chain positions PanMixer's HMM actually steps over — anchors inside blocks holding two or more anchors — is **0.26%**; across **every** variant, which is what model C makes a chain position, it is **4.26%**, with **30,800** records over 25% missing. Worst is the class v1 adds inside blocks PanMixer already chains — the non-anchor records of multi-variant blocks — at **11.31%**, with **7,555** over 25% missing (all measured in this project; the per-class table is in `docs/memory.md`, 2026-09-26). **Each of those means is over a different set of positions; none of them is quotable without naming its set.** The pathological end is bounded: exactly **1 of 340,824** sites has no live donor at all, and **89** leave one or fewer (measured in this project, 2026-09-28).

> **Question** — *"'Backward sampling pass — NEW, nothing to reuse.' Did we check, or did we grep? What did we search for? And '71 MB of alphas' — per haplotype or per subject?"*
>
> On the absence: the claim recorded is that there is **no backward pass and no FFBS anywhere in the PanMixer codebase**, established across the pinned checkout rather than from a single search (measured in this project). The project's own rule is not to conclude absence from a silent grep, so the supporting positive findings are the ones to cite: PanMixer's sampler materialises every dense transition matrix for a block up front, making it **O(A·K²)** in time and memory; the chain runs **within** a block over anchor SNPs with a fresh uniform start state per block and **no** transition model between blocks; and the one reusable piece is the **log-space forward recursion** at `obfuscate.py:140-152`, which is correct and O(K) per step. A codebase with no stored alpha columns cannot have a backward sampler, which is the structural version of the same conclusion.
>
> On the 71 MB: **per haplotype**, for chr21, at K = **88**, for a chain that steps once per block — 100,757 × 88 × 8 bytes ≈ **71 MB** (derived from measured T and K). That is the **model-B fallback** figure. v1's chain under model C steps once per *variant*, which is the ≈ **240 MB** figure in the table above, and under one chain over the chromosome that is resident rather than a ceiling. Both figures are at K = 88 because our target is external and nothing is held out; a head-to-head run at K = 86 would be about 2.3% smaller and any figure quoted from such a run has to say so. Both strands of one subject is twice whichever figure applies, and the 22-chromosome figure **has not been computed in this project**.

### 4.14 The concrete adaptations, spelled out

Collecting the engineering consequences in one place. There are six, not four: the two additions come from the pinned v1 specification of §4.16, which was written after the first version of this list.

1. **Rebuild the BENCHMARK's artifacts so they do not contain the stand-in target.** Two objects have to be kept apart here and the item is worthless if they are merged. **(a) The deployed mechanism.** Our target is external to `G`, `D`, every panel and the attack database, so every artifact the sampler reads — the cohort allele matrix, `support(v)`, the K = 88 state set — is target-free **by construction**. Nothing is held out; there is nothing to recompute. Under model C the mechanism does not read an allele-frequency table or an external panel at all, so the breach below cannot reach it. **(b) The artifacts this project built, and the head-to-head arm that uses them.** Here the finding is real and stands: `get_af.py` branch 1 draws its counts from the 1000 Genomes panel alone, and its own comment asserts that no pangenome subject appears in that panel. That is true of **Phase 3 (0 of 44 HPRC donors)** and **false of the 30x GRCh38 panel we substituted, where 39 of 44 HPRC donors are present, including HG00438** (measured in this project; `docs/memory.md` 2026-09-24, `docs/bugs.md`). So our rebuilt `allele_frequencies.npy` contains the stand-in's own alleles in the frequencies used to score it — a self-inclusion this project introduced while fixing departure (a). For the PanMixer arm that is a stronger version of a self-inclusion it already had; for the **head-to-head**, where a cohort member stands in as target and externality has to be *simulated*, it breaks the simulation. The fix, scoped to that arm: a `loo_cohort` must drop the stand-in from the **1000 Genomes panel** as well as from the pangenome, and must recompute the donor panel, the support counts, the site list and the attack database — not just the donor panel, which is all PanMixer does. There is **no visible symptom** — the frequencies look perfectly normal — which is why it is on this list rather than in a footnote. **What this item used to say, and why it was withdrawn and then re-scoped.** An earlier version said every cohort-derived artifact is target-free *by construction* in our setting and only needed auditing; a later one withdrew that entirely and demanded leave-one-out of everything on our side. Both were wrong in the same place — they did not separate the deployed mechanism from the benchmark. Target-freedom is something the **threat model** gives the mechanism and something a **benchmark** has to be rebuilt and asserted into.
2. **Re-derive the transition constants from the published parameters, not from the code.** Use Ne = 10,000; the recombination-rate constant r = 1.26 (again, unrelated to LD r²); Δx in centiMorgans from a real genetic map; d = Δx · Ne · r, the expected number of ancestral recombinations between the two sites; stay probability p = e^(−d/n) + (1 − e^(−d/n))/n and switch probability q = (1 − e^(−d/n))/n, with n the number of donor states. Do not carry over `EFFECTIVE_N = 1/10,000`, the row-index "distances", or the factor of 4. Without this the chain never recombines — which we measured on 300 of 300 blocks — and R_D degenerates to "copy one donor haplotype verbatim", which would make our tilt meaningless just as it makes theirs inert. **The genetic map this needs has been acquired** (2026-09-27; `external/genetic_maps/chr21.b38.gmap`, verified byte-identical between the SHAPEIT4 and Beagle distributions), so the constant-cM/Mb interim is retired and no number should be produced under it. What the real map brings with it is the p-arm zero-cM plateau and the coordinate cut that answers it (§4.16, §4.16.2, replacement 4).
3. **Normalize the per-variant utility weight into a bounded similarity.** Convert 1/support(v) into the (beta_v, phi_v) pair defined in §4.13 — under model C that is beta_v = w_v/Σw and phi_v an indicator, with no per-block normaliser — so that u lands in [0, 1] by construction rather than by inspection. This is condition 3, and it is the one condition an implementer can silently break: a utility function that is merely "usually small" does not satisfy it, and the theorem gives nothing at all if the range is unbounded. The check is cheap and belongs in the code as an assertion, not in a review comment: assert per position that phi ∈ [0, 1], assert once per run that the beta sum to 1, and assert per draw that log Z_p ∈ [0, eta_tau]. That last one is exit gate 1 (§4.16.5) and it is the cheapest correctness assertion in the whole implementation.
4. **Extend the forward pass to store all its intermediate vectors.** The existing forward algorithm discards alpha_t as it goes because it only needs a scalar likelihood. Backward sampling requires all of them. Under model C that is one chain over the chromosome: ≈ **240 MB** per haplotype at K = 88, resident (derived; ≈ 71 MB under the model-B fallback, which steps once per block; §4.13 has the arithmetic). Store them in **float64** — that is load-bearing, not a preference, because under model C the per-position tilt is only **13.5×** the float32 resolution. To be honest about the size of this change: it is the smallest of the *edits*, not "the single code change that converts PanMixer's scorer into the first half of our sampler". An earlier version of this list said that, and it contradicted item 2 on the same page. The forward pass we need also requires the corrected transition constants (item 2), so what we inherit from `forward_algorithm` is its **log-space stay/switch recursion** — which is correct, and is O(K) per step — not the function. Note what is *not* required of it on our side: no haplotype has to be dropped from the emission panel, because our target was never in it (K = 88). That requirement belongs to the head-to-head arm, where a cohort member stands in.
5. **Emit an auxiliary `REASON` array during VCF conversion.** `VCFtoNP.py` keeps only `fields[1]` (POS) and the `GT` subfield of each sample column, so the entire `INFO` column — `LV`, `PS`, `CONFLICT`, `AT` — and also REF, ALT and the record ID are discarded. The consequence is that the distinct causes of a `-1` become one undifferentiated value before the mechanism ever sees the data (§4.17). On chr21 there are **four** of them, not five: all **237,594** non-piped `GT` fields have exactly one distinct value, `"."`, so there are **zero true haploid genotypes** on this chromosome (measured in this project, 2026-09-28). All four are distinguishable *at conversion time* and none is recoverable afterwards, so this is a small preprocessing addition now in place of an impossible reconstruction later. `docs/plan.md` states it as a v1 prerequisite in four words: *"Cheap now, unrecoverable later."*
6. **Implement the missing-data policy explicitly, and pin it with a test.** An undeclared default here is a silent modelling choice, and it is a choice that changes what the sampler samples. It barely mattered for PanMixer, whose chain positions are anchors with **0.26%** mean missingness; it matters a great deal for v1, where every variant is a position and mean missingness is **4.26%** with **30,800** records over 25% missing — rising to **11.31%** and **7,555** in the worst class, the non-anchor records inside multi-variant blocks (measured in this project; each figure over its own set of positions). The four options and their biases are in §4.17. **The policy is now decided: RENORMALISE** (2026-09-28), and exit gate 4 is that it is implemented and covered by a test that fails if the behaviour changes.

> **Question** — *"Six now, where there were four. Is that the complete list, or is it 'six things we happened to find'? > **Question** — *"Item 2 used to say the genetic map had not been acquired and that the interim was base pairs at a constant cM/Mb rate. What changed, and does a switch mass measured under the real map now mean something?"* The map arrived on 2026-09-27 and the interim is retired, so yes — with one restatement still owed. The old worry was that "realistic separations" is a statement in centiMorgans, so under a constant-rate conversion the thing measured was partly a function of the rate assumed; a real map removes that. What the interim could always do is falsify the degeneracy, and the size of the defect is why: the shipped constants give a total switch mass of **5.8 × 10⁻¹²** at a one-index gap and **5.8 × 10⁻⁶** at a gap of 10⁶ (measured in this project), against **0.13 to 0.99** under the published Fig. 6 constants (quoted from the published paper) — a gap of 10 to 11 orders of magnitude that no plausible cM/Mb rate closes by accident. What is still owed is the gate's threshold: **"order 0.1 to 1" is wrong for adjacent chain positions under model C**, which sit about 12 bp apart, where the correct value is the measured 0.0026. > **Question** — *"Item 4 used to say that extending the forward pass was 'the single code change that converts PanMixer's scorer into the first half of our sampler.' It now says that was wrong. So how many changes is it really, and what exactly do we keep?"*
>
> One control-flow change and three correctness changes. The honest version of the original sentence is narrow: **storing every alpha column is the single change to the forward pass's control flow.** It is not the only change needed before that forward pass is ours.
>
> What else has to change before it is usable (all measured in this project): the panel-and-normaliser mismatch has to go — the shipped code scores against all **88** haplotypes including *its* target while normalising transitions by **86** (`obfuscate.py:130,132`), and whichever count is right, the two must agree. For **our** mechanism the answer is **88 on both sides**, because our target is external and nothing is held out; only in the head-to-head arm, where a cohort member stands in, does the panel drop to 86 — and then the normaliser must drop with it. The transition constants must be re-derived from the published Methods, because `EFFECTIVE_N = 1.0/10_000.0` is the **reciprocal** of Ne = 10,000 and the "distance" is a difference of **VCF row indices** where centiMorgans belong; and the non-anchor fill must go, because its guard never fires and `argmin` always selects the last anchor. What we keep is the **log-space recursion** itself (`obfuscate.py:140-152`), which is correct and O(K) per step.

> **Question** — *"Item 3 says condition 3 'is the one condition an implementer can silently break'. What's the test that catches it? If I hand you a phi_t, how do you check its range?"*
>
> Three assertions, in increasing order of what they catch, and all three are cheap:
>
> 1. **Per block, per draw:** assert 0 ≤ phi_t ≤ 1. This catches a similarity that is not normalised — the commonest way to hand over an out-of-range phi_t is to pass a raw weight like W_t instead of a fraction of it.
> 2. **Once per configuration:** assert that the beta_t sum to 1 to floating-point tolerance. Weights that do not sum to 1 break the **calibration**, not merely the emphasis, so this is not a cosmetic check.
> 3. **Per draw:** assert 0 ≤ log Z_p ≤ eta_tau. This is the composite check — it fails if either of the first two is violated in a way that reaches the sampler, and it is free because log Z_p falls out of the forward pass as the sum of the per-block normalisers.
>
> Together those are exit gate 1's runtime half (§4.16.5). What they do **not** catch is the neighbouring error that matters most: using arctanh(tau) when u spans Δu rather than [0, 1], where the correct calibration is arctanh(tau)/Δu. With the beta_t summing to 1 and every phi_t in [0, 1], Δu is exactly 1 and the issue does not arise — which is why assertions 1 and 2 are what keep assertion 3 meaningful, rather than being subsumed by it.

### In plain words — Section 4, Part B

PanMixer and our mechanism look superficially similar — both use a Li–Stephens haplotype hidden Markov model over the same 88 cohort haplotypes from 44 individuals, both trade privacy against utility — but they do not even cut the chromosome the same way (PanMixer works in linkage-disequilibrium blocks; our chain steps variant to variant and has no blocks in it at all), and they protect different people against different threats and they end at completely different places. PanMixer protects someone who is *inside* the public graph, and its output is the cohort file with that person's genotype column rewritten; ours protects an *external* person's mapped path and never touches the public reference at all. PanMixer picks one candidate per block and then runs a knapsack-style optimization to decide which blocks to replace, leaving the rest exactly as they were; ours has no selection step and resamples the whole path from a tilted distribution. That one difference is decisive: because PanMixer republishes unselected blocks verbatim — about 77% of the 201,514 block-by-haplotype decisions on our chr21 run at capacity 0.1 were never selected, and a further 14,526 of the 47,070 that were selected (30.9%) produced a replacement identical to what the target already carried, so nearer 84% of block-haplotypes are unchanged in fact — two inputs that differ at an unselected block produce outputs whose supports do not overlap at all, so their total variation distance is 1 and no bounded-distinguishability guarantee is possible. That is not a bug in PanMixer, which never claimed such a guarantee; it is a structural fact about any mechanism that passes part of its private input through unchanged, and it is the reason our release rule has to resample everything. The two privacy numbers are also formally incomparable — PanMixer's per-block score is an average-case information quantity and our tau is a worst-case bound on a whole output distribution — so any head-to-head must be run on measured attack success, using a corrected version of their gap-score attack that excludes the target from its maximum, and `docs/plan.md`'s head-to-head exit gate already settles that the correction is applied to **both** arms, with PanMixer's as-published numbers reported alongside so the cost of the correction stays visible. In terms of code, the pinned checkout is about 8,661 lines of Python (measured in this project), and while the data model, the graph-to-matrix preprocessing, the stay/switch forward algebra and the attack half of the evaluation suite are reusable, the sampler, the privacy score, the release rule, the block segmentation and the utility half of the evaluation suite are not — `af_loss` and `ld_loss` measure how far a release moved the cohort's own statistics, which is not defined for an object that never edits the cohort, and the test our guarantee actually makes, two-target indistinguishability, has no counterpart in their suite at all — and the backward sampling pass at the heart of our method does not exist anywhere in their codebase; the only split this project records is a rough one — `docs/memory.md` puts what we inherit at *"roughly 55-65% … by volume, but under 10% of the mechanism"*, a figure recorded in this project by impression rather than by counting, exactly as §4.13 says — and anything more precise would need a per-file accounting nobody has done, though the files that constitute the mechanism can be named without counting anything: `hmm.py`, `get_support_and_pmi.py`, `optimizer.py`, `stacker.py` and `obfuscate.py`. The six concrete adaptations are: rebuild the **benchmark's** artifacts so they never contain the stand-in target — including our own 30x allele-frequency table, which currently does, though the deployed mechanism reads no such table and gets target-freedom from the threat model instead; re-derive the transition constants from the published Li–Stephens parameters with distances in centiMorgans rather than copying the code's constants; normalize the per-variant utility weight into a bounded similarity so that u stays in [0, 1] by construction; extend the forward pass to keep all of its intermediate vectors in float64 so a backward sampling pass becomes possible; emit a `REASON` array at VCF-conversion time so the four distinct causes of a missing genotype are not collapsed into one integer; and implement the missing-data policy, RENORMALISE, pinned by a test. Do those six things and PanMixer's biologically grounded cohort model becomes our target-independent prior, with a formal pairwise identifiability guarantee bolted on top. Part C says exactly what the first version of that will be, which decisions it deliberately leaves open, and the five gates it has to pass — and states plainly that none of it has been run.


> **Question** — *"It closes with 'we have specified and proved on paper but not yet run on data.' What's the first thing we'll run, when will we know whether it works, and what result would kill it?"*
>
> The five exit gates of the v1 specification, in order (§4.16.5):
>
> 1. **Toy enumeration.** The sampler matches exact enumeration; exact TV ≤ tau across a grid of tau and many input pairs; log Z_p inside [0, eta_tau] on every draw; tau = 0 reproduces the baseline exactly. This is the **first** thing to run and it needs no genomic data at all.
> 2. **One code path.** No allele-frequency branch exists in the source, and there is no singleton special case to route around — under model C there are no blocks, so no T = 1 chains and no per-block `Z_b`.
> 3. **Real transition behaviour.** Measured switch mass of order **0.1 to 1** at realistic separations, and a sampled stretch demonstrably **not** a single donor copied verbatim — the gate PanMixer's shipped sampler fails **300 of 300** (measured in this project). **This is the gate that could kill the approach.** ⚠ It is also recorded as **mis-specified as written** and must be rewritten before it is used: adjacent chain positions under model C sit about 12 bp apart, where the biologically correct switch mass is the measured 0.0026, so the gate as stated would reject a correct model.
> 4. **The missing-data policy** — RENORMALISE — implemented and covered by a test that fails if the behaviour changes.
> 5. **No clamped cM survives into the chain.** Every chain position carries a genuine interpolated centiMorgan value; interpolate with `left=nan, right=nan` and assert no NaN remains after the coordinate cut, so the failure is loud rather than silent (see the p-arm cut in §4.16).
>
> *(An earlier draft's gate 5 — "emits `new_haplotypes` so the inherited evaluation suite runs unmodified" — is **not** a gate of the mechanism. It is one option for one comparison and belongs to the head-to-head arm.)*
>
> So: gate 1 tells us the mathematics was implemented correctly, and gate 3 — once rewritten — tells us whether the mosaic the whole utility argument depends on actually materialises on real data. A gate-3 failure, the chain still coming back as single-donor copies over long stretches with correct constants and the real map, would mean the method does not do what it claims at this scale. Everything after that is a utility measurement, and there is currently **no utility measurement at any tau**.

> **Question** — *"'About 8,661 lines of Python' and 'any percentage would be a guess'. Keep the honesty, but can we at least say which files are the mechanism? That's a list, not a percentage."*
>
> Yes, and a list is the better answer. The mechanism lives in five files: **`hmm.py`** (the Li–Stephens machinery and the anchor mapping), **`get_support_and_pmi.py`** (the per-block privacy score, the utility weight and the candidate draw), **`optimizer.py`** (the knapsack LP), **`stacker.py`** (writing selected blocks into the release) and **`obfuscate.py`** (the standalone that runs the whole per-subject pipeline). Everything else in the **8,661 lines** (measured in this project) is data preparation, evaluation and plumbing.
>
> What we replace is in those five files; what we inherit is almost everything outside them — the VCF-to-matrix conversion, the block dictionary, the frequency table, and the whole evaluation suite. The earlier "55–65% by volume, under 10% of the mechanism" figure is an **estimate with no counting method** and should be quoted as such or not at all. The file list above is assembled from the files this project's own defect records name as the locations of the scorer, the sampler, the optimizer and the release rule; **no per-file accounting exists in this project**, so treat it as a map of where to look rather than a measured partition.

### 4.16 What v1 actually is

> **Key idea** — the scope decision, as `docs/plan.md` now records it. The original wording was *"maximise reuse of PanMixer and change exactly ONE thing, the sampler — keep its blocks, its data model, its donor panel and its whole evaluation suite."* **That went too far and was narrowed on 2026-09-28**, because it imported PanMixer's threat model — in which the target is a cohort member — into a design whose target is external to `G`, `D`, every panel and the attack database. What we reuse is PanMixer's **DATA** and its **forward recursion**. Its blocks as a chain unit, its donor-panel-minus-target, and its cohort-distortion metrics are **comparison-arm choices, not properties of our design.**

> **Key idea** — **the chain architecture is MODEL C** (decided 2026-09-28): *one chain over the whole chromosome, with the individual variant as the chain unit.* Model B — one chain whose units are LD blocks — is retained as a **fallback** if the PI wants closer parity with PanMixer. Model A — independent per-block chains with no transitions between blocks — is **ruled out**; it is 11× too much recombination and its fresh-uniform restart per block is algebraically the claim that consecutive blocks are infinitely far apart genetically. Note for anyone reading an earlier draft: what the original spec pinned was **model A**, described as PanMixer's code behaves rather than argued for on its own terms. Do not describe the thing that was pinned as "the block-structured variant (model B)" — that mislabels the fallback.

**What choosing C removes from the project**, and it is the reason it was chosen: with the individual variant as the chain unit there are **no LD blocks in the mechanism**, hence **no PLINK run and no external panel in the mechanism's input set at all**; **no anchor concept**, hence **no PanGenie callset**; and **no allele-frequency table**. C also deletes the singleton special case — there are no T = 1 chains, no per-block `Z_b`, one forward pass and one backward sample. **It is simpler than what was pinned, not harder.** The external panel survives in only one place: the **attack database** in the evaluation, where cohort overlap must be removed before it is loaded.

Three measurements decided it, all on chr21 with the acquired GRCh38 map and the published constants (Ne = 10,000, r = 1.26). The analytic segment length for reference is n/(Ne·r) = 0.0068 cM, about **4.5 kb**.

| model | chain | E[donor switches] | mean segment | alphas at K = 88 | status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** per-block chains, no inter-block transitions | 100,757 separate | 104,379 | 0.39 kb | 71 MB | **RULED OUT** |
| **B** one chain, units = **blocks** | 100,757 | 3,532 | 11.60 kb | 71 MB | fallback |
| **C** one chain, units = **variants** | 340,824 | 7,155 | 5.73 kb | 240 MB | **DECIDED** |

*(Switch counts, segment lengths and the analytic reference all measured in this project, 2026-09-28; the alpha volumes are derived from T × K × 8 bytes at K = 88, which is our state count because the target is external.)*

> **Defect** — **the p arm, and how v1 handles it.** This is a real property of chr21 that the chromosome-wide chain runs straight into, and the handling is a stopgap, so it is written down here to be revisited rather than forgotten. **What the p arm is:** chromosome 21 is *acrocentric* — its centromere sits very near one end, so the short ("p") arm is a small stub made almost entirely of repetitive satellite DNA and ribosomal-RNA gene arrays rather than unique single-copy sequence. In GRCh38 that stub is not really assembled: chr21 5.01–10.81 Mb is **placeholder model sequence with 25 fabricated N-gaps**, and 1000 Genomes' strict accessibility mask rules **99.98%** of the variants there inaccessible. Our own cohort matrix is **36.76% missing** across that region against **6.57%** elsewhere. Genetically it is also inert: deCODE's pedigree map assigns **0.0 cM/Mb** to every 1 Mb bin from 0–13 Mb, and a four-generation pedigree observed **zero** p-arm allelic crossovers in **107** transmissions (all measured or quoted as recorded in this project, 2026-09-28).
>
> **Why that breaks a chromosome-wide chain specifically.** `np.interp`'s clamping below the map's first marker, plus the map's own zero-cM centromeric plateau, give **34,345 consecutive positions with identical cM**. Identical cM means Δx = 0, means d = 0, means `P(switch) = 0` at every one of them — so the sampler would copy **one donor's real haplotype verbatim across 7.25 Mb**. That is departure (b), the failure we criticise PanMixer for, at roughly **200×** the scale of their worst block, and it would arrive in our own output.
>
> **How v1 handles it:** a **coordinate cut**. The chr21 chain runs over **12,968,320 < POS <= 46,680,243**, keeping **305,887 of 340,824** variants (**89.75%**) and dropping everything outside (decided 2026-09-28; the telomeric end closed 2026-09-29).
>
> ⚠ **REVISIT — this is a pragmatic cut to get v1 running, not a settled answer.** Maps that nominally cover the region exist (pyrho hg38 from 5,088,754 bp; the Eagle redistribution carries 66 markers there) but spend ~10 cM across intervals defined by two markers, i.e. interpolation across a void — which would manufacture recombination rather than measure it. Two smaller zero-distance defects also remain unaddressed: **592 variants above the map end**, a 593-position zero-distance run at the telomere, and **618 interior zero-cM map intervals** holding about **1,650** kept variants. The interior plateaus are real and correct to leave; the telomeric run is not, and needs a decision. Anything done about the p arm later should be done after the base mechanism runs, not instead of running it.

That scope decision is doing more work than it looks. It is a deliberate refusal to fix everything at once. The build mismatch, the anchor definition, the utility metric's blindness to base pairs and the gap-score attack's missing exclusion are all real, all recorded, and all *out of scope for v1* except where the sampler cannot run without them. The reason to scope it this way is that a single changed component can be attributed — with the caveat that the narrowing above means fewer components are shared than the original wording implied.

> **Question** — *"Why build a v1 that knowingly keeps four defects in it? If the blocks come from the wrong build and the anchor set is misnamed, aren't we building on sand?"* Because three of the four never reach our mechanism at all, and for the last one an attributable difference is worth more right now than a fully corrected pipeline. One at a time. The **build mismatch** lives in an artifact — the allele-frequency table binned against GRCh37 intervals — that model C does not read; it still has to be repaired for the **PanMixer arm**, and it has been: the 30x GRCh38 rebuild gives **258,610** strict (POS, REF, ALT) matches on chr21 against **1,025** on Phase 3 (measured in this project). The **block boundaries** are likewise not in our input set. The **anchor definition** is not inherited either; replacement 1 deletes the concept. What genuinely remains is one **evaluation-side** item, the utility metric's indifference to how many base pairs a change rewrites — plus the gap-score attack's max-over-non-target form, which `docs/plan.md`'s head-to-head exit gate already settles by applying the published form to **both** arms with PanMixer's as-published numbers reported alongside (§4.16.8, item 3). So "four defects kept" overstates it badly: it is one, in the measuring instrument rather than in the mechanism.

> **Question** — *"If v1's numbers come out worse than PanMixer's, how do I know it was the sampler and not our own 30x repin?"* You do not know it from the numbers — you know it from running both arms on the same graph, the same cohort and the same attack evaluators, and from being honest that under the narrowed scope **fewer components are shared than the original wording claimed**: the chain unit, the donor-panel size and the utility axes all differ by design, not by accident. Two threats to attribution are already recorded and neither is hypothetical. First, our rebuilt `allele_frequencies.npy` contains the stand-in target's own alleles, because **39 of 44** HPRC donors are present in the 30x GRCh38 panel including HG00438, against **0 of 44** in Phase 3 (measured in this project) — that is a benchmark defect, and adaptation 1 of §4.14 is the fix; the deployed mechanism reads no frequency table and is untouched by it. Second, the gap-score attack takes its maximum over the whole attack database with no exclusion, so in *their* setting a target can be its own rank-1 hit (measured in this project; recorded severity HIGH). Neither is a reason to distrust the design; both are reasons the head-to-head has to be run with a properly simulated externality on the stand-in before any difference is attributed to any component.

> **Caution** — **our mechanism runs at K = 88.** The target is external and was never in the graph, so no haplotype has to be removed, and condition 1 holds by construction. **n = 86 is not a v1 choice; it is a benchmark artefact.** It arises in the head-to-head arm only, where a cohort member stands in as target so that externality can be simulated, and simulating it means dropping that stand-in's two haplotypes. Any number that depends on the state count must say which setting produced it. Our own alpha-storage figures are all at K = 88: ≈ **240 MB** for model C's one-position-per-variant chain over chr21, ≈ **71 MB** under the model-B fallback (both derived). A head-to-head figure at 86 would be about 2.3% smaller and must be labelled as a comparison-arm number.

**A question this immediately raises.**

> **Question** — *"n = 86 is 2(N−1) with 'the target's own two haplotypes removed'. But our target is external — there is nothing of his in the panel to remove. So whose two haplotypes are we dropping?"* **In the deployed mechanism, nobody's: K = 88, unconditionally.** Your instinct is exactly right and it is the thing the earlier draft of this primer got wrong. The 86 belongs to one place only — the **head-to-head arm**, where there is no external target available on PanMixer's data, so a **cohort member stands in** as target and externality has to be manufactured by removing that stand-in's two haplotypes. Every measurement of this kind in this project used subject **HG00438** as the stand-in (measured in this project). So the two numbers are not "the entitlement and the elected comparison"; they are *the mechanism* and *a simulation of the mechanism on a dataset that cannot supply a real external target*. ⚠ Even a full leave-one-out is only an **approximation** of the external-target setting, because the PGGB graph **topology** was built from all HPRC assemblies, so `G` itself saw the stand-in. That is a limit of the comparison arm, not of our design: with a real external target the graph never saw them. What `docs/` does **not** record is any run of either kind, so no number anywhere in Part C is a RanPanMixer result.

#### 4.16.2 What v1 replaces, and the evidence that forced each one

**1. Drop the anchor concept entirely. Every variant is a chain position.**

An anchor, in the released code, is a pangenome record that also appears in the PanGenie `bi_all` callset, matched exactly on (POS, REF, whole-ALT-string) — not the 1000 Genomes subset the published Methods define. v1 has no such notion: the chain steps over every variant on the chromosome, and **there is no PanGenie callset in our input set at all** (measured 2026-09-28: it has exactly two live consumers, both of which our design deletes, and no evaluator reads it).

The evidence that forced it is the **anchor blindness** recorded in Section 3 (§A.4, and departure (d) of Section 3's *Where the implementation departs from the paper*). Its shape is this: PanMixer's privacy score, on the HMM branch, builds its observation vector and its emission panel from the anchor positions **only**, so a non-anchor variant is not a chain position and contributes nothing whatever to the likelihood. Worked case, block 1083 on our chr21: two common anchors at allele frequency 0.934 plus one non-anchor at **0.00385**. The all-variant allele-frequency sum would give **5.6956 nats**; what the code returns is **0.0349 nats** — a factor of **163×** (measured in this project; `docs/bugs.md`). Across 400 randomly chosen HMM-branch blocks the median score is **1.079 nats** against an all-variant median of **2.769 nats**, and in **32%** of blocks the rarity that was ignored exceeds the score itself.

The general lesson is what transfers to us, not the specific number: **any quantity computed only at anchors is a statement about the anchors, not about the block.** We are not computing a privacy score at all, so we do not inherit that defect — but our *utility* is a per-block quantity, and if it were computed only at anchors then our output would be indifferent to whether it reproduced the target's rarest material. The same blindness, on the other axis.

Two further facts make the anchor set hard to defend as a "well-behaved position" filter, and they are the reason the decision was to drop the concept rather than to re-define it. The code's anchor set (**273,475** records) and the paper's `V_SNPs` — top-level SNPs, **253,000** records — overlap in **234,532**, so **38,943 anchors (14.2%) are not top-level SNPs at all**, of which **13,436 are nested variants** — precisely the class the paper's own stated rationale excludes — while **18,468 qualifying top-level SNPs (7.3%) are not anchors**. The PanGenie overlap is a proxy for "well-behaved" that is neither necessary nor sufficient (all measured in this project; `docs/memory.md` 2026-09-25).

And the rationale that does *not* survive contact with the data is compute. Anchors number 273,475 against 340,824 total variants, only **1.25×** more positions — **24.1 million** operations against **30.0 million** at K = 88 (measured in this project). Nothing about the anchor restriction is a performance decision.

> **Note** — what we *lose* by dropping anchors is real and is the subject of §4.17. The honest justification for the anchor rule is neither compute nor multi-allelic emissions but **missingness**: on chr21, top-level SNPs are **2.25%** uncalled on average and nested variants are **22.53%** — about ten times as often (measured in this project). Anchors are a crude way of keeping the badly behaved positions out of the chain. v1 lets them in and then has to decide what to do with them.

**A question this immediately raises.**

> **Question** — *"Missingness at chain positions goes from 0.26% to 4.26% the moment we drop anchors, and the records we add inside multi-variant blocks run 11.31%. Is this actually a win, or have we swapped a measurement defect for a data-quality problem?"* Both statements are true, and they sit on different axes, which is why the trade is not a wash. Anchor blindness is a defect in what a *number means*: a quantity computed only at anchors is a statement about the anchors, and on block 1083 the two readings are **0.0349 nats** against **5.6956 nats**, a factor of **163×** (measured in this project). Missingness is a property of the *data*, and dropping anchors does not create it — the **11.31%** mean and the **7,555** positions over 25% missing were always in the matrix (measured in this project); the anchor rule kept them out of the chain, which is precisely how the honest justification for that rule reads. So what changes is that a problem previously handled by exclusion now has to be handled by modelling, and §4.17 is the bill. It is also why RESTRICT — putting only PanMixer's positions in the chain — stays on the menu as a **comparison baseline** rather than being dismissed: it is the one option whose behaviour has been published. The policy we adopt **is** decided — **RENORMALISE**, 2026-09-28 — and §4.17 gives the two measurements that closed it.

> **Question** — *"So the code's anchor set is not the paper's set. Does that mean the paper's rationale is fine and only the implementation drifted, or is the rationale bad too?"* Split verdict, and the split is measured. The published rationale — restrict the chain to well-behaved top-level SNPs — is **supported** on the axis that matters: on chr21, top-level SNPs are **2.25%** uncalled on average and nested variants **22.53%**, about ten times as often (measured in this project). The released implementation's *proxy* for that rationale is the PanGenie overlap, and the proxy is neither necessary nor sufficient: **38,943 anchors (14.2%)** are not top-level SNPs at all, of which **13,436** are nested variants — the class the paper's own rationale excludes — while **18,468 qualifying top-level SNPs (7.3%)** are not anchors (all measured in this project). So the statement this project is required to make is the narrow one: the released code selects a different set than the published rationale describes. We have reproduced none of the published figures and are not claiming any of them would move. And the reason this ends in deletion rather than repair is the standard you set yourself in the working session — a component whose definition cannot be defended is a component to drop — and that conclusion holds whichever anchor set is "better", a question this project has not answered.

**2. One code path. Under model C there are no blocks, so there is no singleton case, no T = 1 chain and no per-block `Z_b`. There is no allele-frequency branch at all.**

In the released code a block is routed to one of two mechanisms. This is what forced the change:

- The **routing thresholds disagree between scoring and sampling.** Scoring takes the HMM branch when a block has **≥ 1** anchor; sampling takes it when the block has **≥ 2**. On chr21 that is **10,954** blocks scored by the HMM against **10,502** sampled by it, so **452 blocks** are scored one way and sampled the other — a block gets an HMM-derived privacy score for a replacement that was drawn from independent allele frequencies (all measured in this project; `docs/bugs.md`). Severity is recorded as MEDIUM. One sampler with one threshold removes the question rather than answering it.
- The **allele-frequency branch draws variants independently**, so it can emit an allele combination that no cohort haplotype carries. That is not merely inelegant: our output support is supposed to contain cohort-supported paths, and an independent per-variant draw is not one.

What the single code path buys is coverage. Under v1 **all 340,824 chr21 variants are cohort-supported**, meaning every one of them gets its allele from a donor haplotype drawn by the tilted sampler. The figure the specification quotes for the share today is **73.9%** (derived; it is the complement of the **26.1% of variants** that sit in single-variant dictionary entries, and those singletons are exactly what the independent allele-frequency draw handles). Name the denominators, because three different percentages live in this neighbourhood and they are not interchangeable: **88.4% of dictionary entries** are singletons, those singletons hold **26.1% of variants**, and the strictly haplotype-modelled share is narrower still — **10.4% of entries** carry two or more anchors and they hold **71.4% of variants** (all measured in this project).

It also disposes of an argument rather than working around it. The published Methods justify the ≥ 2 threshold like this, quoted from the published paper: *"dependent on the number of top-level SNPs that exist inside the LD block. If 2 or more exist, enough SNPs exist to build an HMM, and we can synthesize more accurate new haplotype blocks. If only one top-level SNP exists, we do not have enough useful information to build an HMM."* That is correct **for inferring a chain** — a Markov chain needs two positions for there to be a transition between them. It does not apply to **copying donors**, which is what a Li–Stephens model does: with one position and 88 donors there is nothing to infer and nothing degenerate about drawing one of the 88 and taking whatever allele it carries. Under model C the argument does not even need answering, because the chain does not restart per block: every variant is just the next step of one chromosome-wide chain.

> **Question** — *"So what happened to the T = 1 case — the single-variant block drawn as one tilted categorical? Is that still in the design?"* **No, and model C is what removed it.** An earlier draft made a virtue of routing a single-variant block through the same sampler as a chain of length one; under model C there are no blocks, so there is no singleton class, **no T = 1 chain, no per-block `Z_b`, one forward pass and one backward sample**. What survives is the algebra that made that case easy, and it is now the *general* case: `phi_v` is an **indicator** — 1 if the drawn donor carries the target's allele at v, else 0 — and `beta_v` = w_v / Σ_u w_u with w_v = 1/support(v). Donors that match are multiplied by exp(eta_tau · beta_v) and donors that do not by 1, at every position. This is the sense in which C is **simpler than what was pinned, not harder**. It is also worth seeing how small that tilt is: under the uniform allocation used for illustration in §4.10.4, at tau = 0.5 and T = 340,824 positions the matching donors are favoured by a factor exp(0.549306/340,824) = **1.0000016** (derived). ⚠ **The total tilt exp(eta_tau) is invariant in T** — more positions each move less and they compose to the same total — but the *per-position* tilt under C is only **13.5×** the float32 noise floor, against **45.7×** under model B, which is why float64 stored alphas are load-bearing rather than a preference.

> **Question** — *"You say their ≥ 2 rule is 'correct for inferring a chain'. But their thing isn't inferring a chain either — it's also copying donors. So is their stated rationale simply wrong?"* Care is needed here, and the care is a standing project rule rather than politeness. What can be said: the published sentence justifies the threshold in terms of having enough SNPs to *build* an HMM, which is a true statement about inference; a Li–Stephens copying model at one position infers nothing, so on our reading the threshold is not required for the operation the code actually performs. That is an **argument**, not a measurement, and it licenses no claim that a published number would change if the threshold were removed — we have reproduced none of their figures. What **is** measured is narrower and does not need an opinion about the rationale at all: the released code's two branches use two different thresholds, **≥ 1** anchor for scoring and **≥ 2** for sampling, so **452** of chr21's blocks are scored by one mechanism and sampled by the other (measured in this project; recorded severity MEDIUM). That is a released-code-versus-published-Methods discrepancy, and it is the concrete thing the single code path removes.

**3. Exact tilted forward-filtering / backward-sampling, in place of forward-only ancestral simulation.**

The upstream forward pass keeps only a **running** `log_alpha`: it overwrites the vector at every step and returns one scalar log-likelihood. That is everything it needs for scoring and it is not enough for sampling.

Here is exactly why the omission is fatal to exactness rather than merely inconvenient. The backward pass draws Z_T from alpha_T, and then, walking leftwards, draws Z_t from weights proportional to **alpha_t(i) · A_{t+1}(i, Z_{t+1})**. The weight depends on two things: the forward vector at position t, and the state *already sampled* at position t+1. So the required quantity is not known until the backward walk reaches position t, and by then a running forward pass has overwritten alpha_t many times over. There are only three ways out. Store every alpha column, which is what v1 does — at model C's one-position-per-variant chain that is 340,824 × 88 × 8 bytes ≈ **240 MB** of alpha over the whole chromosome in **float64** (derived), and because C is **one** chain rather than one per block, that is genuinely resident rather than a ceiling over independent pieces. Or re-run the forward pass from the start for every position, which multiplies the work by T. Or give up conditioning on the future and simulate forwards only, drawing Z_{t+1} from the transition out of Z_t — which is a perfectly ordinary thing to do and is **not a draw from Q_p**, because it never accounts for the tilt further along the chain. The third option is the one the released code is structured for, and it is exactly the trap the retired `viterbi_release` was: output that looks entirely normal and carries no guarantee.

What we keep from their code is the **log-space stay/switch recursion** itself, which is correct and O(K) per step.

> **Question** — *"Show me exactly where the tilt gets lost in forward-only simulation. I want to see the term that goes missing."* It is the conditioning that goes missing, not a term. The exact backward draw at position t uses weights proportional to **alpha_t(i) · A_{t+1}(i, Z_{t+1})**, and two things are packed in there: alpha_t already carries every tilt factor at positions 1 through t, and the factor A_{t+1}(i, Z_{t+1}) carries the influence of the state already drawn to the **right**, which was itself drawn under the tilt at positions t+1 through T. Forward-only simulation draws Z_{t+1} from A(Z_t, ·): it looks only leftwards, so nothing at position t+1 or beyond ever influences the draw at t. What you get is the baseline walk with the tilt applied as you pass it — a perfectly well-defined object, and **not** Q_p. Since the bound of §4.7 is a statement about Q_p, an approximate draw carries no bound at all; that is the defect the retired `viterbi_release` had. It is also why the cheapest correctness check in the implementation is an assertion rather than a report: `docs/terms.md` pins **0 ≤ log Z_p ≤ eta_tau** for every input path and says of it, *"Assert it, do not report it."* That is exit gate 1 (§4.16.5).

> **Question** — *"'240 MB, never all resident' was the old answer and it depended on one chain per block. Under model C there is one chain. So what is actually resident?"* **About 240 MB, and now it is the peak rather than a ceiling** — which is the one place model C costs more than the alternatives, and it is worth stating plainly rather than hedging. The whole-chromosome alpha volume is 340,824 × 88 × 8 bytes ≈ **240 MB** per haplotype at K = 88, in float64, for one chain held end to end (derived). After the coordinate cut the chain is 305,887 positions, so the figure is ≈ **215 MB** (derived); quote whichever matches the run. The model-B fallback would be ≈ **71 MB** at T = 100,757 (derived). The scale to compare against is measured: PanMixer's full shipped run costs **68 s and 1.7 GB** peak resident per subject-chromosome on chr21 (measured in this project). A resident 240 MB, inside a pipeline that already tolerates 1.7 GB, is not where this design will run into trouble — and float32 is not the saving to reach for, because under C the per-position tilt is only **13.5×** the float32 resolution.

**4. Re-derive the transition constants from the published Methods rather than from the code.**

The measured consequence of the four together: executing the shipped class on a 44 × 200 × 2 synthetic panel gives a total switch mass of **5.8 × 10⁻¹²** at a one-index gap and **5.8 × 10⁻⁶** at a gap of 10⁶, and the sampled block equalled exactly one donor row. With the published Fig. 6 constants the same quantity would be **0.13 to 0.99** — a gap of **10 to 11 orders of magnitude** (measured in this project; `docs/bugs.md`). On real chr21 data, **300 of 300** randomly chosen HMM-path blocks came back identical to a single donor haplotype.

**A question this immediately raises.**

By how much, and whether either alone crosses gate 3's threshold of order 0.1 to 1, is exactly what the ablation would tell us, and nothing here substitutes for it.

**5. No knapsack.**

Every block is resampled from the tilted distribution, and **nothing is released verbatim by default**. There is no selection vector, no capacity, no LP relaxation and no rounding. Three consequences follow directly, and the third is a reporting rule rather than a code change.

The disjoint-support argument of §4.12 cannot arise, because it needed a category of blocks that pass through unchanged and v1 has no such category. The saturation behaviour goes with it — at capacity 0.5 the shipped run spends only 33.1% of its budget because every positive-value move already fits (measured in this project) — because tau is not a budget to be spent. And the no-op accounting problem disappears rather than being fixed: there is no privacy credit to book, so there is nothing for a move that changed nothing to claim.

> **Caution** — with no selection step, the denominator for every utility number is **every block in the chain**: **Path A**, never **Path B**. The two measurement paths, and why a Path-B number is a different quantity rather than a conservative one, are set out once in the Evaluation part of §4.13.

**A question this immediately raises.**

> **Question** — *"If nothing is released verbatim, then at tau = 0 the output ignores the target completely. Isn't that just PanMixer's removal baseline? Are our tau = 0 arm and their strongest baseline the same thing?"* Same role on a utility curve, different object, and the difference should be kept. At tau = 0 the tilt factor is exp(0) = 1 at every position, so Q_p = R_D for **every** admissible input and the release carries no dependence on the target whatsoever; exit gate 1 (§4.16.5) requires exactly that — *tau = 0 reproduces the baseline model exactly*. That makes tau = 0 the natural left-hand anchor of any tau sweep, and the right place to compare against a removal-style arm. It is not the same object as theirs, though: PanMixer's `to_empty` strategy blanks the subject's column entirely and is the paper's *"Removed"* arm, whose published figures are Wasserstein AF divergence **0.027** and pairwise LD L1 **0.017** (both quoted from the published paper; **we have reproduced neither**). Ours emits a cohort path drawn from R_D rather than emitting nothing. So a tau = 0 number of ours would not be interchangeable with their 0.027 — and no tau = 0 number of ours exists, because no tau has been run.

#### 4.16.3 Why per-block scope would be safe: the proof sketch, retained for the fallback

> **Key idea** — **model C does not need this argument.** With one chain over the whole chromosome, Theorem 1 applies to the whole path directly: there is nothing to factorise, no per-block `Z_b` to multiply together, and no product set to worry about. The section is kept because the **model-B fallback** would need exactly this argument, and because model A — independent per-block chains, which is what an earlier draft actually pinned — is the design this argument was written to defend. Read it as a contingency, not as a description of what v1 does.

Part A's theory is written for one chain, and so is v1. An earlier design ran one chain **per block**: 100,757 independent tilted samplers, one per dictionary entry. It is worth being suspicious of that, because it looks like the kind of scope reduction that quietly weakens a theorem — and if each block got *its own* tau, it would. So here is the argument, in six steps, that it does not.

**The claim.** Drawing each block independently from its own per-block tilted distribution produces a draw from exactly the same global tilted distribution Q_p that Part A's theory is about. Not an approximation of it; the same distribution.

**Step 1 — assume the baseline factorises over blocks.** R_D(y) = R_1(y_1) · R_2(y_2) · … · R_T(y_T), where y_b is the part of the output in block b and R_b is the baseline restricted to that block. For a Li–Stephens copying model with a fresh start state per block and no transition model between blocks, this holds by construction — it is what "per-block HMM" means.

**Step 2 — write out the global tilt.** By definition, Q_p(y) = R_D(y) · exp(eta_tau · u(p, y)) / Z_p, and the utility is the block-weighted sum u(p, y) = Σ_b beta_b · phi_b(p_b, y_b).

**Step 3 — the exponential of a sum is a product of exponentials.** exp(eta_tau · Σ_b beta_b · phi_b) = Π_b exp(eta_tau · beta_b · phi_b). This is the only algebra in the argument and it is the whole reason the tilt survives factorisation: a sum in the exponent becomes a product outside it.

**Step 4 — pair each baseline factor with its own tilt factor.** Substituting Steps 1 and 3 into Step 2:

> **Q_p(y) = [ Π_b R_b(y_b) · exp(eta_tau · beta_b · phi_b(p_b, y_b)) ] / Z_p**

Each bracketed term now involves only block b. Call the sum of that term over all possible y_b the per-block normalizer Z_b.

**Step 5 — the global normalizer is the product of the per-block ones.** Z_p is the sum over all outputs y of the numerator. If the output support is a **product set** — if choosing y_1 does not restrict what y_2 may be — then a sum over all y of a product of per-block factors is the product of the per-block sums: Z_p = Π_b Z_b. Substituting that into Step 4,

> **Q_p(y) = Π_b [ R_b(y_b) · exp(eta_tau · beta_b · phi_b(p_b, y_b)) / Z_b ] = Π_b Q_b(y_b)**

and each factor Q_b is precisely the per-block tilted sampler. A product of independent draws from the Q_b is a draw from Q_p. That is the claim.

**Step 6 — check that Theorem 1 still applies to Q_p unchanged.** The theorem never used any property of the chain's shape. It used exactly the three conditions of §4.8 that the theorem itself needs — a fixed output support chosen without consulting the target (condition 1), a baseline learned from public data only (condition 2), and a utility bounded in [0, 1] (condition 3). Our fourth condition, one release per genome with sampling rather than maximising, is operational and is untouched by how the chain is scoped. Boundedness is where the per-block weights earn their keep — each phi_b ∈ [0, 1] and the beta_b are non-negative and **sum to 1 across all T blocks**, so u = Σ beta_b · phi_b is a weighted average of numbers in [0, 1] and therefore lies in [0, 1]. Hence TV(Q_p, Q_q) ≤ tanh(eta_tau) = tau, for any two admissible inputs, as before.

> **Key idea** — **per-block and chromosome-wide are a scope choice, not a weaker guarantee.** The same tau, the same theorem, the same bound. What the chromosome-wide chain buys is *modelling* — it can copy a donor across a block boundary, which a per-block chain structurally cannot — and not a different privacy statement. That is the reason model C was chosen and model A ruled out, and the reason is about recombination, never about tau.

Two things that are easy to get wrong here, because they are the natural readings and both are false:

> **Caution** — per-block scope does **not** give each block its own tau. If it did, the per-block tilt factor would be exp(arctanh(tau)) — for tau = 0.5, a factor of 1.7321 — instead of exp(arctanh(tau) · beta_b), which under uniform weighting on chr21 is exp(0.5493/100,757) = 1.0000055 (derived). That would be a completely different and much more useful regime, and it is **not** what any of the three models is. ⚠ And nothing about the budget changes between them: `eta_tau` is identical and the weights still sum to 1 over the whole path, so the thin-budget arithmetic of §4.10.4 applies to model C exactly as written — at C's T = 340,824 positions the per-position factor is exp(0.5493/340,824) = **1.0000016** (derived), smaller per position and identical in total. **The total tilt exp(eta_tau) is invariant in T.** No choice of chain unit escapes the §4.10.4 tension; it only redistributes it.

> **Caution** — Step 5 is the step with a real weak point, and it is measured. It needs the output support to be a **product set** — blocks that do not constrain each other. On our chr21, block disjointness does not hold cleanly: **31,487 records (9.2%)** have a start position lying inside an earlier record's span, **15,540 of 100,757 blocks (15.4%)** contain such a record, **78,937 variants (23.2%)** live in an affected block, and **1,229 of 10,502 HMM-path blocks (11.7%)** are affected (all measured in this project; `docs/memory.md` 2026-09-22). The ledger's own verdict is that this is *"not documentable as an edge case … so block disjointness — which both the knapsack's additive accounting and our FFBS chain assume — fails broadly, not locally."* Where it fails, the product of per-block draws can assert a combination of a parent record and its children that no single traversal of the graph realises. That does not break Theorem 1 — the theorem is about distributions over whatever the support is — but it does mean **the support may contain outputs that are not graph-valid paths**, which is a separate promise we might want to make and have not yet made. It is open question 6 of `docs/plan.md`, and it is one of the reasons the definition of a chain position is reserved (§4.16.8).

**Two questions about the argument and the caveat above.**

> **Question** — *"Let me say Step 5 back to you. As long as the blocks don't constrain each other, sampling each block on its own is the same as sampling the whole path, and the only thing I give up is that a donor can't be copied across a block boundary. And then the Caution says the premise is false on our data — so is the argument broken or not?"* Three clauses; the verdict is right, right, and no. **Right** that per-block draws reproduce the global tilted distribution exactly when the support is a product set — that is Steps 1 through 5, and the only algebra in it is that a sum in an exponent becomes a product outside it. **Right** that what you give up is modelling reach and not the bound: a chromosome-wide chain can copy one donor across a boundary and a per-block chain cannot, which is a statement about which outputs are likely, not about how distinguishable they are. **But no**, the overlap measurement does not break the argument, and it is worth being exact about what it does break. The factorisation is exact over whatever support we declare, and Theorem 1 is likewise a statement about distributions over whatever the support is. What fails is a **different** promise: with **31,487 records (9.2%)** starting inside an earlier record's span, **15,540 of 100,757 blocks (15.4%)** containing such a record and **78,937 variants (23.2%)** living in an affected block (all measured in this project), the product of independent per-block draws can assert a parent-plus-children combination that no single traversal of the graph realises. Whether our support must contain only graph-valid paths is `docs/plan.md` open question 6, and it is unanswered.

> **Question** — *"If the guarantee is identical either way, why ever widen the chain? Could we just declare per-block scope permanent and be done?"* What widening buys is modelling, and this primer should not dress that up as a privacy argument: a chromosome-wide chain can carry one donor across a block boundary and can therefore reproduce linkage that spans boundaries, which a per-block chain structurally cannot. That is now a **decided** question rather than an open one, and it was decided on measured recombination, not on the bound (2026-09-28). The numbers, against an analytic segment length of n/(Ne·r) = 0.0068 cM ≈ **4.5 kb**: independent per-block chains (**model A**) give **104,379** expected donor switches on chr21 and a mean segment of **0.39 kb** — 11× too much recombination, because a fresh uniform restart at every block boundary is algebraically the claim that consecutive blocks are infinitely far apart. Measured truth is the opposite: median inter-block switch mass **0.0022** against the **0.9884** that independence implies, with **98.5%** of block pairs below half of it, and consecutive blocks as tightly linked as adjacent variants within one (median gap **1.52e-05 cM** versus **1.81e-05 cM**). **Model B** — one chain, blocks atomic — discards the within-block half of the recombination, **51%**; block sizes are median 1 and p90 3, so the loss concentrates in the tail, and the largest block, **618** variants over **39.5 kb**, is one the correct model recombines through **73%** of the time and which B would emit as a single donor verbatim. **Model C** lands nearest the analytic length at **5.73 kb** and **7,155** switches, and is the correct Li–Stephens model. Blocks are a poor chain unit on this data regardless: **89,087 of 100,757** dictionary entries (**88.4%**) are orphan variants that fell in no called PLINK block, so a "block boundary" is mostly a bookkeeping outcome rather than a recombination site (all measured in this project, 2026-09-28). What remains reserved is narrower: the tandem-duplication definition of a position at hypervariable sites (§4.16.7, §4.16.8, item 1).

#### 4.16.4 The constraint this imposes on the utility function

The general requirement is that the utility must factor **along the chain positions**, because that is the only shape the forward recursion can carry. **Under model C it is satisfied by construction and there is nothing to check**: a chain position is one variant, `phi_v` is an indicator on that variant, and u = Σ_v beta_v · phi_v is additive by definition. The constraint below is therefore a statement about *candidate utilities we might reach for later*, and about the **model-B fallback**, where a step covers a whole block and a similarity really can fail to decompose.

> **Definition** — a per-step similarity phi_b **decomposes along chain positions** if it can be written as a sum over the positions t inside step b of terms that depend only on position t: phi_b(p_b, y_b) = Σ_{t ∈ b} w_t · s(p_t, y_t), with the w_t summing to 1 inside the step. Anything of that shape becomes a per-position potential the forward recursion can carry.

`docs/plan.md` states the constraint in one line: *"phi_t must decompose along the chain positions inside a block. A whole-block similarity that does not decompose cannot be sampled exactly by ffbs."*

The kind of measure this rules out is worth naming concretely, because the primer has already offered one. §4.5 illustrates phi_t with a **population-weighted k-mer weighted Jaccard** between the target's block sequence and the candidate's. As a similarity between two sequences that is a perfectly reasonable choice. As a per-block potential it is unusable, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of implementation effort: a k-mer of length k that straddles the boundary between two chain positions depends on the alleles at **both** of them at once. So the score cannot be written as a sum of per-position terms, the forward recursion has no potential to multiply in at each step, and FFBS cannot draw from the tilted distribution exactly. What one would get instead is an approximate sampler — and an approximate draw from Q_p carries no bound, for the same reason `viterbi_release` carries none.

The same objection applies to any measure defined on an **assembled** stretch: edit distance between two block sequences, alignment score, a length-weighted overlap of structural alleles. Each couples positions. The (beta_v, phi_v) construction of §4.13, by contrast, lives at a single variant, so it decomposes trivially — which is a point in its favour that had nothing to do with why it was proposed.

> **Open question** — whether a non-decomposing similarity can be *approximated* by a decomposing surrogate closely enough to be worth using is not settled here, and nothing about it has been measured. The safe statement is the narrow one: v1's utility is the indicator-and-1/support construction, and the **k-mer weighted Jaccard is DEFERRED to a post-build evaluation and reporting tool** — it is not v1's utility and it is not a live design constraint. There is consequently no decision on k, no weighting function and no decision on set versus multiset semantics, and none is needed before the sampler is written.

**Two questions this immediately raises.**

> **Question** — *"So the k-mer Jaccard of §4.5 is unusable. Then why is it in the primer at all — should we just cut it?"* Keep it, and change what it is for. Two separate objections apply to it and they have different consequences. The structural one is this subsection's subject: as a potential **inside** the sampler it cannot be used, because FFBS can only multiply in factors that live at one chain position, and an approximate draw from Q_p carries no bound. The practical one is that it needs **sequence** — k-mers are strings — while v1's data model is an int16 allele-index matrix with no sequence in it at all, so computing it would mean going back to the graph for node sequences. Neither objection touches its use as a metric **reported after** a release, where nothing has to factor and the graph is available — and that is now where it lives: **deferred to a post-build evaluation and reporting tool**. So the honest status is: v1's phi is the indicator-and-1/support construction of §4.13, and the k-mer measure is a reporting instrument to be built once there is a release to report on.

> **Question** — *"Why can't I just widen the state to cover k positions at once? Then the straddling k-mer is local again."* You can widen the state, and it does not rescue this particular measure. Carrying the previous k−1 alleles in the state turns an order-k dependence into a first-order chain over an augmented state space, at the cost of multiplying the state count by the number of allele combinations across those k−1 positions — at our K = 88 panel, a factor of up to 88^(k−1) in the worst case (derived), which is fatal at even modest k and would throw away the "compute is not a constraint" conclusion of §4.16.6 for nothing. But the deeper obstruction is not the straddle. A **weighted Jaccard is a ratio whose denominator is a union over the whole block's k-mer multiset**, so its value depends on the alleles at every position through the normalisation, not only on the k−1 neighbours, and no finite state augmentation localises that. Note the limit of this answer: `docs/plan.md` records the *requirement* — *"A whole-block similarity that does not decompose cannot be sampled exactly by `ffbs`"* — and **no formal impossibility proof for the Jaccard case is recorded in `docs/`**. What is recorded is the constraint that v1's utility must decompose.

If it does not — if **88** HPRC haplotypes at chr21 separations simply do not recombine at a useful rate — then our output is one donor's haplotype copied verbatim over long stretches, the tilt has almost nothing to act on, and the design needs rethinking rather than debugging. The genetic map it needs **is now in hand** (`external/genetic_maps/chr21.b38.gmap`, acquired 2026-09-27), so the gate can at last be run against the published transition model rather than against a constant-rate fallback — which is also how the p-arm zero-cM plateau was found in the first place (§4.16).

**Two questions about the gates.**

> **Question** — *"What is the first number I will actually see, and what value of it would tell me this does not work?"* The first numbers come from gate 1, on a graph small enough to enumerate every output, and there are four of them: the sampler's empirical distribution against exact enumeration; the **exact** TV between release distributions against tau, across a grid of tau and many input pairs; log Z_p against its bracket [0, eta_tau] on every draw; and whether tau = 0 reproduces the baseline exactly. One trap to name before reading any of them: `docs/terms.md` records that a plug-in TV estimate over a large support is **severely biased upward** at finite sample size, so an estimate above tau on a big support is the expected behaviour of the estimator and **not** a violation of Theorem 1 — *"Only exact enumeration on a toy support can falsify Theorem 1."* Gate 1's TV check is meaningful precisely because the support is tiny. The number that could kill the approach is gate 3's switch behaviour — **read against a restated threshold**, since the gate's "order 0.1 to 1 between adjacent positions" is mis-specified for a 12 bp separation and would reject a correct model. The value that kills it is one where, with correct constants and the real map, the chain still produces single-donor stretches: that would mean **88** HPRC haplotypes at chr21 separations do not recombine usefully and the tilt has almost nothing to act on. None of these numbers exists yet; no value of tau has been run end to end, and **no RanPanMixer number exists**.

> **Question** — *"Gate 2 says 'no allele-frequency branch exists in the source' and calls itself a grep. Why is a grep an exit gate rather than a code-review note?"* Because the defect it guards against has no symptom. It is the defect that produced the **452** mis-routed blocks: two code paths whose thresholds drifted apart, where nothing failed and nothing errored — blocks were simply scored by one mechanism and sampled by the other (measured in this project). A defect with no symptom needs a check that does not depend on anybody noticing. Under model C the gate simplifies: there is no second branch to route to and no singleton class to assert about, so all that is left is the absence claim. For that, one discipline from `docs/bugs.md` applies directly. Its recorded rule is never to conclude a string is absent from extracted PDF text on the strength of a silent grep — GNU grep treats a file containing a NUL byte as binary and prints nothing, indistinguishable from no matches, and that is how a false "not present" conclusion about someone else's paper once got recorded as fact. The rule was earned on a PDF rather than on source code, so generalising it to our own tree is our inference rather than a recorded finding — but it is the right instinct, and gate 2's absence claim should be confirmed rather than inferred from empty output.

#### 4.16.7 Hypervariable sites: the hardest case and the clearest opportunity

The single hardest input our sampler will face is one specific site on chr21, and it is worth setting out in full here, because it is simultaneously the worst case and the place where a path-level guarantee should show its advantage most clearly. The site is `grch38#chr21:14569980` — in graph terms the bubble `>102270111>102277685`, at snarl level **LV = 0**, meaning it sits on the GRCh38 backbone path rather than inside another variant (measured in this project; `docs/memory.md` 2026-09-22).

Its measured properties, all on our chr21 rebuild: **90 declared alleles**, of which **88 distinct alleles are carried across the 88 cohort haplotypes — every haplotype unique**; a REF allele of **257,439 bp**; a single VCF line of about **28 MB**; **2,464 nested child records**, so **2,485 matrix rows (0.7% of chr21's 340,824)** describe this one region; and the region spans **370** overlapping PLINK blocks. Its class is not a singleton: chr21 carries **7** sites with ≥ 88 alleles, **155** with ≥ 44, and **618** with ≥ 20 (all measured in this project).

**Why it is the hardest case for us.** Three separate assumptions strain at once, and none of the three has been resolved:

- **Is there a valid traversal to emit at all?** Our output support is supposed to contain graph-valid paths. Even a single chain that steps through these 2,485 rows one at a time can produce a parent/child combination that no haplotype carries, because the transition model constrains *which donor* is copied, not *whether the parent allele admits the child bubble*. Model C makes this materially better than model A did — the 2,485 rows are now consecutive steps of one chain with real transitions between them, and the stay probability at 12 bp separations is very close to 1, so the chain will usually hold one donor through the whole region rather than reshuffling 370 times — but "usually" is not "provably", and whether our support can be restricted to valid traversals is `docs/plan.md` open question 6, unanswered.
- **What is one position here?** This region is one top-level record plus 2,464 children. Under the segmentation PanMixer works in it straddles **370** overlapping PLINK blocks, and under model A that was 370 chains that knew nothing about each other — which is precisely why A was ruled out. Under model C the block count is irrelevant; the region is 2,485 consecutive positions of one chain. What model C does **not** solve is the tandem duplication: one allele at this site, allele 58, is a **whole-region tandem duplication** walking 10,008 node steps over 5,014 distinct nodes, so about 4,994 nodes are visited twice. For such a path, *"the allele of path p at position t"* is not a well-defined phrase, and both u_path and phi_v assume it is. **This is the part of the chain-position question that is still reserved**, and `docs/plan.md` flags it as needing settlement before the sampler is written.
- **Should phi be length-weighted?** Changing one allele here rewrites up to a quarter of a megabase — the largest single rewrite measured in PanMixer's own capacity-0.1 run on this subject was **257,485 bp** — while `1/support(v)` charges it the same as a one-base substitution. A utility that counts records is indifferent to that. Whether our phi should weight by sequence length is undecided (§4.16.8).

**Why it is also the opportunity.** PanMixer's privacy score for this record is effectively zero, and the mechanism by which that happens is instructive. The record does get a sensible allele frequency — each of the target's two alleles there has f_v = **1.540e-4**, worth **8.78 nats** of self-information, the rarest material on the chromosome. That value is **never used**: the record is not an anchor, its block takes the HMM branch, and on the HMM branch the forward algorithm scores only the anchors. So the most identifying record on chr21 contributes nothing to the quantity that drives selection. (Measured in this project; `docs/memory.md` 2026-09-24, Correction 8. Note for anyone who read an earlier draft: this is **not** the `−log 0` zeroing — that branch exists in the code but does not fire on our data, where **0 of 319,092** checked sites have f_v = 0. It is a completely different failure.) It was nonetheless rewritten in that run: the target's alleles **[89, 88]** were replaced by **[35, 64]** on both strands at capacity 0.1 (measured in this project).

Our bound behaves differently here, and the difference is structural rather than a tuning advantage: **tau does not degrade with rarity.** The (1 + tau)/2 bound is quantified over all admissible input pairs, so it covers the pair that differs only at the most identifying site on the chromosome exactly as tightly as it covers a pair differing at a common SNP. There is no ranking, no threshold and no per-site score that could be blind to this record. That is the sense in which a path-level worst-case guarantee is the right instrument for hypervariable structural sites.

> **Caution** — that is an argument about the guarantee, not a measurement of our mechanism, and the mechanism does not exist yet. Nothing in `docs/` measures our behaviour at this site or any other. It is also not free: the three unresolved items above must be settled before the advantage can be claimed, and the third of them — how to avoid double-counting a top-level record against its own 2,464 children in both the utility and any privacy measurement — has no proposed answer at all.

**Two questions this immediately raises.**

> **Question** — *"Allele 58 walks 10,008 node steps over 5,014 distinct nodes. If the sampler draws a donor that carries that allele at the parent record, what do the 2,464 children get? Concretely — what integers land in the matrix?"* The answer changed with model C, and it is worth seeing why the old answer was worse. Under the per-block design the **2,485** matrix rows describing this region sat in **370** PLINK blocks (measured in this project) drawn by **370 independent** chains, so the donor at the top-level record had no influence whatever on the donors at the other 369 blocks' rows — the integers that landed were whatever those independent draws produced. Under **model C** the region is 2,485 consecutive steps of **one** chain: the transition model connects them, adjacent positions here are a few base pairs apart, and the stay probability at that separation is very close to 1, so the chain will normally carry a single donor through the whole region and emit that donor's actual parent-and-children combination. That is a real improvement and it is still **not a guarantee**: nothing forbids a switch mid-region, and a switch between a parent record and its own children can assert a combination no haplotype carries. Whether our output support can be *restricted* to graph-valid traversals is `docs/plan.md` open question 6, unanswered. And the harder half is untouched by the architecture: for a path that visits about 4,994 nodes twice, the phrase *"the allele of path p at position t"* does not pick out one thing, and both u_path and phi_v assume it does. `docs/plan.md` flags that as needing settlement **before** the sampler is written — it is what remains reserved in §4.16.8, item 1.

> **Question** — *"'tau does not degrade with rarity' sounds too good. What is the catch?"* Three catches, and none of them is a hole in the bound. First, it is a property of the guarantee and not a measurement: nothing in `docs/` measures our mechanism's behaviour at this site or at any other, because the mechanism does not exist. Second, a uniform bound on *disclosure* says nothing about achievable *utility* here — under model C and the uniform allocation used for illustration in §4.10.4 this region holds 2,485 of 340,824 chain positions, **0.73%** of the utility budget (derived) for **0.7%** of the chromosome's rows (measured in this project), so the guarantee covers the site as tightly as anywhere while the mechanism's ability to reproduce the target's alleles there is bounded by the same thin tilt as everywhere else. Third, the three unresolved items above must be settled before the advantage can be claimed, and the third of them has no proposed answer at all. So the accurate form of the claim is comparative and narrow: PanMixer's per-block score is structurally blind at this record and our bound is structurally not — a difference in the instrument, not a demonstrated advantage in a release.

#### 4.16.8 What v1 deliberately leaves open

Each of these is undecided **on purpose**. A future draft of this primer that quietly resolves one of them has introduced an error, not removed one. Two items that used to head this list are now **closed** and are recorded here so nobody reopens them by accident: the chain architecture (**model C**, decided 2026-09-28) and the missing-data policy (**RENORMALISE**, decided 2026-09-28).

1. **What counts as a position at a hypervariable site — the part of the T question that survives.** The architecture is settled: model C, one chain, one position per variant, T = **340,824** on chr21 before the coordinate cut. What is **not** settled is what a position means where a path visits the same node twice. `docs/plan.md` still carries it as part of open question 8, raised by you on 2026-09-22 and reserved for a conversation **with your PI**, and the sub-problem is concrete: at `chr21:14569980`, allele 58 is a whole-region tandem duplication visiting about 4,994 nodes twice, so *"the allele of path p at position t"* does not pick out one thing, while both u_path and phi_v assume it does (§4.16.7). Also still live, and related: whether the output support can be restricted to graph-valid traversals at all (`docs/plan.md` open question 6), for which snarl-tree levels remain the leading route — they would make the 257 kb region **1** position instead of the 370 overlapping blocks it straddles, and would make positions disjoint by construction. **Who decides: you and your PI.**
2. **The coordinate cut.** The chain runs over chr21 **12,968,320 < POS <= 46,680,243**, keeping **305,887 of 340,824** variants (**89.75%**), because below the start the map's zero-cM plateau plus `np.interp` clamping give **34,345** consecutive positions with identical cM and hence `P(switch) = 0` across 7.25 Mb (§4.16). ⚠ **Flagged for revisit — a pragmatic cut to get v1 running, not a permanent answer.** The **592** variants above the map end were the same defect in miniature (a 593-position zero-distance run) and were cut on the same principle on 2026-09-29. What remains is **618** interior zero-cM intervals holding about **1,650** kept variants — real map plateaus, and correct to leave.
3. **Whether PanMixer's arm also gets the max-over-non-target correction — on this list because it is easy to assume the wrong answer, not because it is undecided.** The released gap-score attack takes its maximum over the entire attack database with no exclusion of the target, while published equation (7) specifies a maximum over the non-target individuals; because HPRC individuals largely appear in 1000 Genomes, a target can be its own rank-1 hit in **their** setting (measured in this project; `docs/bugs.md`, recorded severity HIGH). This one **is** answered in the plan: the exit gate for `[A-EVL-headtohead]` requires the correction *"applied to BOTH arms, with PanMixer's as-published numbers also reported so the cost of the correction is visible"*. ⚠ Be careful how it is described on our side. Our target is external to the attack database, so this is **not** a leave-one-out of cohort membership for us — but that does not mean "nothing is excluded on our side". A linkage evaluation is only defined if the attacker holds the target as a candidate, so the max-over-non-target form may well be the right estimator for our arm too, as a property of **the metric** rather than of who is in the cohort. Standing constraint on writing any result from this up: the narrow claim is that the released code differs from published equation (7); we have reproduced none of the published figures and cannot say how much any published number would move.
4. **The remaining genetic-map defects.** The GRCh38 map itself **has been acquired** (`external/genetic_maps/chr21.b38.gmap`, 2026-09-27, verified byte-identical between the SHAPEIT4 and Beagle distributions), so this is no longer an acquisition task and the constant-rate fallback is retired. What remains are the zero-distance regions in item 2 and the restatement of exit gate 3, whose "order 0.1 to 1 between adjacent positions" threshold is wrong for a 12 bp separation (§4.16.5). **Who acts: us.**
5. **The beta allocation scheme.** Uniform, weight-proportional (w_v = 1/support(v)), or length-weighted — undecided. The thin-budget arithmetic of §4.10.4 assumes uniform purely for illustration. The constraint that survives whatever we choose: the scheme must be fixed **without looking at the target**, or condition 2 fails and the theorem gives nothing. **Who decides: us, and it must be pre-registered.**
6. **What value of tau is defensible.** `tau = 0.10` bounds equal-prior identification at **0.55** (derived). Which tau is acceptable is a **policy question we can inform but cannot settle**, and `docs/plan.md` records the discipline either way: pre-register it before any attack outcome is inspected. **Who decides: not us alone.**

> **Question** — *"Let me check I have the shape of the T decision. It isn't 'how long should the chain be' — it's 'what counts as a position', and fixing that fixes T, which fixes how thin the per-position tilt is. Right?"* Right, and the first half is now answered. `docs/plan.md` puts the principle in one line — *"T is not a free parameter — it is fixed by what we declare a 'position' to BE"* — and the answer adopted on 2026-09-28 is **one position per variant**, giving T = **340,824** on chr21. It was chosen on measured recombination, not on the bound: model C's mean segment of **5.73 kb** lands nearest the analytic n/(Ne·r) = 0.0068 cM ≈ **4.5 kb**, against **11.60 kb** for blocks-as-units and **0.39 kb** for independent per-block chains (all measured in this project, 2026-09-28). The consequence runs straight into §4.10.4 exactly as you say — the per-position tilt under uniform allocation is exp(eta_tau/T), which at T = 340,824 and tau = 0.5 is **1.0000016** (derived) — but note that the **total** tilt exp(eta_tau) is invariant in T, so a larger T redistributes the budget rather than shrinking it. What is **not** answered is what a position means at a tandem duplication, and that is item 1 above. **Who decides that: you and your PI.**

> **Question** — *"Item 5 says the beta scheme has to be fixed without looking at the target. But a weight-proportional beta is built from `support(v)`, and support is counted over the cohort. Doesn't that already disqualify it?"* **For our mechanism, no — and this is exactly the place the old answer imported PanMixer's threat model.** `support(v)` is a count over the 88 cohort haplotypes, our target is **external** to that cohort, so a weight-proportional beta looks only at public cohort data and never at the target. Condition 2 is satisfied by construction; nothing has to be held out, and `support(v)` needs no recomputation at all — the VCF's own `INFO/AN` field is bit-identical to `np.sum(pangenome != -1, axis=(0,2))` across all 340,824 chr21 records (measured in this project, 2026-09-28). The concern *is* real in two other places. In **PanMixer's** setting the released support counts include its target (`obfuscate.py:208`), which is correct for their threat model and is part of what departure (c) describes. And in **our head-to-head arm**, where a cohort member stands in as target, the stand-in's two haplotypes must come out of the support counts along with everything else, or the simulated externality is not simulated. Which of uniform, weight-proportional or length-weighted beta we adopt stays undecided; the constraint that survives whichever we pick is that it be fixed, and pre-registered, without consulting the target.

### 4.17 The missing-data policy: four options, and why RENORMALISE was chosen

**The policy is decided: RENORMALISE** (2026-09-28). A donor that is missing at a position is dropped from the state distribution there, and the remaining donors are renormalised. The four options are still set out below, because a chosen policy whose alternatives are not understood is a policy nobody can defend, and because RESTRICT is retained as a **comparison baseline**. Two measurements closed the decision: only **1 of 340,824** chr21 sites has all 88 donors missing, and **89** sites leave one donor or fewer (measured in this project, 2026-09-28) — so the pathological case RENORMALISE would mishandle, a position with nothing left to sample from, is vanishingly rare and can be handled by an assertion rather than by a policy. What remains is implementation plus **exit gate 4**: the behaviour pinned by a test that fails if it changes.

**Why it became a decision.** PanMixer's chain positions are anchors, and anchors are barely ever uncalled: mean missingness at the anchors its HMM steps over is **0.26%**. v1 makes every variant a chain position, and mean missingness across every variant is **4.26%**, with **30,800** records over 25% missing; the class v1 adds inside already-chained blocks is worse still, **11.31%** with **7,555** over 25% (all measured in this project; per-class table in `docs/memory.md`, 2026-09-26). That is a **16-fold** increase (derived, 4.26 / 0.26) in how often the sampler meets a donor with nothing to copy, and it turns a negligible implementation detail into a modelling choice that shapes which donors get copied. That is the measured reason the decision matters more for us than for them.

**Why one policy is being asked to do too much.** A `-1` in the matrix is **four different conditions wearing one value** on chr21. They do not want the same treatment.

| Cause of `-1` | What it actually means | How common on chr21 (measured in this project) |
|---|---|---|
| **not applicable** | The child bubble sits inside a parent allele this haplotype does not carry. **The DNA does not exist on that chromosome.** | **66.1%** of nested missingness |
| **inherited** | The parent call was itself missing, so the child could not be called either | **12.1%** of nested missingness |
| **assembly gap** | The donor's assembly does not cover the region. The allele exists; we do not know it | **349** runs of ≥ 100 consecutive missing positions, covering **448,283** entries |
| **conflict** | The sample had multiple graph paths that disagreed, so `vg` wrote `.` | **1,855** records (0.54% of chr21), mean **29.4 of 88** haplotypes missing at such a site |
| ~~**haploid genotype**~~ — **does not occur** | Would be a VCF giving one allele where two were expected, with the converter writing `-1` into strand 1 | **Zero on chr21.** An earlier draft counted **3.135%** of sample `GT` fields as haploid; re-checked 2026-09-28, all **237,594** non-piped `GT` fields have exactly one distinct value, `"."`, so every one of them is a missing call and **none is a true haploid genotype** (measured in this project) |

The first row and the third row are the crux. "Not applicable" means the donor genuinely has no allele to copy, and dropping it from consideration at that position is *correct*. An assembly gap means the donor almost certainly has an allele and our data simply does not record it, and dropping it there **penalises poorly-assembled donors for reasons that have nothing to do with genetics**. A single policy cannot be right for both.

**The four options.** Options are columns; read across a row to compare them on one axis.

| | **RENORMALISE** | **WILDCARD** | **MISMATCH** | **RESTRICT** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What it does at the position** | Drops that donor from the state distribution at that position and renormalises over the donors that remain | Treats missing as matching whatever the target carries | Treats missing as a difference from the target | Removes the position from the chain altogether, for every donor |
| **What it silently assumes** | That `-1` means "this donor has no allele here" — i.e. that every `-1` is *not applicable* | That `-1` means "this donor might well carry what the target carries" | That `-1` means "this donor carries something other than what the target carries" | That a position where anyone is missing carries no information worth modelling |
| **The bias it introduces** | Correct for not-applicable; **wrong for an assembly gap**, where it penalises badly-assembled donors for reasons unrelated to genetics | Makes poorly-assembled donors **universally attractive** — the emptier a donor's assembly, the better it scores | Penalises assembly gaps as though they were **genuine variation**, inventing differences the data never asserted | Shrinks the chain back toward the anchor regime, discarding the rarest material along with the badly behaved positions |
| **Effect on T** | None — every position stays in the chain | None | None | **Changes T**, because positions leave the chain. It is therefore not merely an emission rule |
| **Effect on the tilt budget** | None directly | None directly | None directly | Fewer positions to spread beta over, so a slightly larger share each |
| **Provenance** | Your leaning from 2026-09-25, **decided 2026-09-28** | An option on the menu | An option on the menu | **This is what PanMixer's anchor rule does in effect**, and it is kept deliberately as a baseline **because it is the published behaviour**, on your explicit instruction (2026-09-25) |
| **Status** | **DECIDED — the v1 policy** | not chosen | not chosen | retained as a comparison baseline, not as a default |

> **Note** — the RESTRICT column is on this list for a specific and good reason, and it is not "for completeness". If v1's results differ from PanMixer's, we want to be able to say whether the difference came from the sampler or from the fact that v1 puts all **340,824** chr21 variants into chains while PanMixer's chains step only over anchors — **273,475** anchor records on chr21, and only those sitting inside a block with two or more of them are actually chain positions (both measured in this project). A RESTRICT run is the arm that isolates that: same sampler, PanMixer's position set. It is the only option here whose defence is *"we know what this does, because it has been published"*.

The same property that makes RESTRICT useful as a baseline is what makes it dangerous to compare against.

> **Caution** — because RESTRICT changes which positions are in the chain, a run under RESTRICT and a run under RENORMALISE are **not comparable on any per-position or per-block utility number**, even at the same tau. `docs/terms.md` states the rule flatly: valid comparison is *"another run under the SAME policy. Never compare across policies."* The policy therefore belongs in the configuration stamp attached to every result, next to the graph and cohort identity, tau, and the utility identity.

**How the leaning became the decision.** `docs/plan.md` and `docs/terms.md` recorded **RENORMALISE** as a leaning from 2026-09-25, with the reason for waiting stated in your own words — *"I'm going to have to understand the missingness case more before I decide anything but right now I'm leaning towards renormalize."* The understanding arrived as two measurements on 2026-09-28. First, the degenerate case is essentially absent: exactly **1 of 340,824** chr21 sites has all 88 donors missing and **89** sites leave one donor or fewer, so "renormalise over what is left" almost always has plenty left, and the rare exception is an assertion rather than a policy problem. Second, one of the five supposed causes does not exist on this chromosome at all — there are **zero** true haploid genotypes — which removes one of the cases a single rule was being asked to cover. That does not make RENORMALISE right for an assembly gap; it makes the residual risk small enough to take, and the split-by-cause option stays available behind the `REASON` array. Exit gate 4 (§4.16.5) is now implementation: the behaviour pinned by a test that fails if it changes.

**The prerequisite for revisiting it well.** The four causes are distinguishable **at conversion time** and at no point afterwards:

- `LV` and `PS` — the snarl level and parent-bubble id — separate *not applicable* from *inherited*.
- Run length separates *assembly gaps*: on strand 0 across all 44 subjects there are **28,052** runs of consecutive missing positions with median length **1** and maximum **23,478**, and **54.4%** of runs are isolated single positions, which is the structural signature rather than the gap signature (measured in this project).
- The `CONFLICT` INFO tag names *conflicts* outright.
- The arity of the `GT` field would have identified a *haploid genotype*, if any existed — on chr21 none does: all **237,594** non-piped `GT` fields are `"."` (measured in this project, 2026-09-28). Keep the check for other chromosomes; expect it to fire nowhere on this one.

And every one of those is discarded by `VCFtoNP.py`, which keeps only `fields[1]` (POS) and `fields[9+i].split(':')[0]` (the `GT` subfield) — the whole `INFO` column goes, and so do REF, ALT and the record ID. By the time anything reaches the matrix all four causes are the same integer. Hence the prerequisite written into the v1 specification: **v1 preprocessing should emit an auxiliary `REASON` array beside the allele matrix**, which keeps a cause-aware policy available without reopening the decision. `docs/plan.md` gives the argument in four words — ***"Cheap now, unrecoverable later."***

> **Open question** — whether the policy should later be **split by cause** (renormalise a not-applicable, treat an assembly gap as something closer to a wildcard, and so on) is recorded as a recommendation in `docs/memory.md` and is not part of v1. It is the option the `REASON` array exists to keep open, and it is the only option on the table that does not force one answer onto all four causes. **Who decides: you, after v1 runs.**

**Two questions this immediately raises.**

> **Question** — *"Let me say back where this lands. RENORMALISE is right for 'not applicable' and wrong for an assembly gap; WILDCARD makes badly assembled donors universally attractive; MISMATCH invents differences the data never asserted; RESTRICT changes T and is basically the published behaviour. And the split-by-cause option is the only one that doesn't force one answer onto all the causes. Have I got it, and which way did the evidence point?"* Every clause is right, and the evidence pointed to **RENORMALISE**, which is now the decision. Here is the balance as it stood. **For a cause-blind policy:** the largest single cause of a missing genotype is the one where dropping a donor is simply correct — *not applicable* is **66.1%** of nested missingness (measured in this project) — so one rule is right for the plurality of cases; and one of the five supposed causes turned out not to occur on chr21 at all, there being **zero** true haploid genotypes among **237,594** non-piped `GT` fields (measured in this project, 2026-09-28). **Against it:** the assembly-gap cause is real and structurally different, **349** runs of ≥ 100 consecutive missing positions covering **448,283** entries (measured in this project), and there the donor almost certainly has an allele we merely failed to record, so dropping it penalises poorly-assembled donors for reasons unrelated to genetics. That residual bias is what we are accepting, and the `REASON` array is what keeps a split policy reachable later. **Against a split policy today:** it cannot be implemented at all until that array exists, because `VCFtoNP.py` has already destroyed the distinction by the time the mechanism runs. **On comparing runs:** RESTRICT changes which positions are in the chain, so it is not comparable with the others on any per-position number even at the same tau, and `docs/terms.md` is flat about it — valid comparison is *"another run under the SAME policy. Never compare across policies."* Exit gate 4 is now implementation: RENORMALISE, covered by a test that fails if the behaviour changes.

> **Question** — *"11.31% mean missingness in the class v1 adds, and 7,555 records over a quarter missing. At one of those, RENORMALISE leaves me sampling from roughly three-quarters of the panel. Does the tilt still work? Does phi still lie in [0, 1]?"* The tilt and the bound are untouched; the machinery is not. In order: under model C `phi_v` is an **indicator** at a single variant, so its range is [0, 1] by definition however many donors are live at that position — condition 3 constrains the *range* of the utility, not the state set, and Theorem 1 needs nothing more. What changes is the code. At a position where a quarter of the donors are uncalled the live state set is about three-quarters of the panel (derived), so the transition normalisation has to use the **live** count rather than a constant K, and the running total that makes the stay/switch collapse O(K) per step has to be recomputed at that position rather than carried forward. The collapse itself survives — it only ever needed one stay probability, one switch probability and a running total over live states, and **the transition matrix is never materialised** in any case, since `A = (1−s_t)·I + (s_t/K)·J` is rank-one plus diagonal — so the O(T·K) cost of §4.10.2 is preserved. That interaction is the thing to write into the sampler deliberately, and it is the specific reason the missing-data policy is not a detail that can be left to a default. The failure case it has to guard is now bounded by measurement: exactly **1 of 340,824** chr21 sites has no live donor at all, and **89** leave one or fewer (measured in this project, 2026-09-28).

### In plain words — Section 4, Part C

Part C is the specification we are going to build. Its governing idea was originally a deliberate refusal to fix everything at once — keep PanMixer's blocks, its data model, its 86-haplotype donor panel and its entire evaluation suite, and change only the sampler — and that wording was **narrowed on 2026-09-28**, because it imported PanMixer's threat model into a design whose target is external to the graph, the cohort, every panel and the attack database. What we actually reuse is PanMixer's **data** and its **forward recursion**. Our donor panel is the full **K = 88**, because nothing is held out when the target was never in there; the 86-haplotype panel belongs to the optional head-to-head arm, where a cohort member stands in as target and externality has to be simulated. Its cohort-distortion metrics, `af_loss` and `ld_loss`, are not ours at all: they measure how far a release moved the cohort's own statistics, and our release never edits the cohort, so they are **not defined for our release object** — not zero, undefined — while the test our guarantee actually makes, two-target indistinguishability, has no counterpart in their suite. The chain architecture is **model C**: one chain over the whole chromosome, stepping variant to variant, decided on measured recombination (its 5.73 kb mean segment lands nearest the analytic 4.5 kb, against 11.60 kb for blocks-as-units and 0.39 kb for the independent per-block chains that were originally pinned and are now ruled out). Choosing C deletes a great deal: no LD blocks in the mechanism, hence no PLINK run and no external panel in its inputs; no anchors, hence no PanGenie callset; no allele-frequency table; and no singleton special case — no T = 1 chains, no per-block normaliser, one forward pass and one backward sample. It is simpler than what was pinned, not harder. The utility collapses with it: phi at a position is just an indicator of whether the drawn donor carries the target's allele there, and beta is that position's share of the total 1/support weight, bounded in [0, 1] and additive by construction. Forward-only simulation becomes exact forward-filtering/backward-sampling, which means storing every forward vector — about 240 MB at K = 88 over chr21, genuinely resident now that there is one chain, and in float64 rather than float32 because the per-position tilt is only 13.5 times the float32 resolution. The transition constants are re-derived from the published Methods (Ne = 10,000, r = 1.26, distance in centiMorgans, d = Δx · Ne · r) rather than copied from code that uses the reciprocal of Ne and a difference of VCF row indices, and the GRCh38 map that requires has now been acquired and verified. The knapsack goes, so nothing is released verbatim and there is no selection pattern to leak. One real cost comes with the chromosome-wide chain and is handled rather than hidden: chr21's short arm is an unassembled, genetically inert stub where the map's zero-cM plateau and interpolation clamping give 34,345 consecutive positions with identical cM, so the switch probability is exactly zero and the sampler would emit one donor's real haplotype across 7.25 Mb; v1 cuts the chain to 12,968,320 < POS <= 46,680,243 — closed at the telomeric end too, where 592 variants above the map's last marker formed the same defect in miniature — keeping 89.75% of chr21's variants, and that cut is explicitly a stopgap to revisit once the base mechanism runs. Compute is not the constraint anywhere in this — about 30 million operations for a whole chromosome. What is genuinely unsettled is narrower than it used to be: what a position means where a path visits the same node twice remains reserved for you and your PI, the p-arm treatment is flagged for revisit, and the beta allocation scheme and the value of tau are still to be pre-registered. The missing-data policy is now **decided — RENORMALISE**, with the `REASON` array kept so a cause-aware policy stays reachable, and with one of the five supposed causes of a missing genotype turning out not to occur on chr21 at all. Five exit gates say when v1 is done: the toy enumeration matches exact calculation with log Z_p in range on every draw and tau = 0 reproducing the baseline; one code path with no allele-frequency branch in the source; the chain demonstrably recombining rather than copying one donor verbatim — the gate that could kill the approach, and the one whose stated threshold must be restated before it is used, since "switch mass of order 0.1 to 1" is wrong for positions 12 bp apart; the missing-data policy pinned by a test; and no clamped centiMorgan value surviving into the chain. Emitting `new_haplotypes` is **not** a gate — it is one option for one comparison. **None of this has been run, no value of tau has been executed end to end, and no RanPanMixer number exists.**

> **Question** — *"The close says none of it has been run. Can it also tell me which parts are proved, which are only specified, and which are neither?"* Three piles. **Proved on paper:** Theorem 1 and its bound TV(Q_p, Q_q) ≤ tanh(eta_tau) = tau; the corollary that any attacker's accuracy at equal priors is at most (1 + tau)/2, which at tau = 0.10 is **0.55** (derived); and — for the model-B fallback, which v1 does not use — the factorisation of §4.16.3. Under model C no factorisation is needed: one chain, one application of Theorem 1 to the whole path. **Specified but not implemented:** the v1 sampler itself, the (beta_v, phi_v) construction of §4.13, the `REASON` array, the p-arm coordinate cut, and all five exit gates. **Neither proved nor measured:** every utility number and every attack number for our mechanism at every tau, because **no tau has been run and no RanPanMixer number exists**; the switch mass under corrected constants, which is now computable since the genetic map is in hand but still needs gate 3 restated; and the decisions that remain reserved — what a position means at a tandem duplication, the beta allocation scheme, the value of tau, and whether the p-arm cut is the permanent answer. Every number in Part C that is a measurement is a measurement of PanMixer or of the data, never of us.
